Used Breakroom and Cafeteria Seating

Stack chairs, cafe chairs, stools and lounge pieces pulled from corporate breakrooms and graded honestly. You see the condition before you buy, so there are no surprises when the pallet arrives.

6 minute read

Breakroom seating is one of the easiest places to save money when you furnish an office. The chairs in a corporate cafeteria are built for heavy daily use, they are usually bought in large matching quantities, and they come out of liquidations in far better shape than most people expect. Buying used breakroom seating lets you fill a lunchroom, a coffee bar or a training space with commercial-grade chairs instead of retail furniture that will wobble within a year.

This page explains what kinds of seating you will find, how our condition grades apply to breakroom pieces, what to inspect, and how to plan quantities so your space works on day one.

Types of breakroom and cafeteria seating

Inventory changes with every decommission, but most corporate breakroom seating falls into a few categories.

Stacking and cafe chairs

The workhorse of the corporate cafeteria. Look for polypropylene or molded plastic shells on steel frames, sometimes with a fabric or vinyl seat pad. They stack for cleaning and for flexible room setups, which matters if your breakroom doubles as an all-hands space.

Counter and bar stools

Used at coffee bars, window counters and high-top tables. Seat height matters here. Counter stools usually suit surfaces around 36 inches tall, and bar stools suit surfaces around 42 inches. Measure your counter before you pick a stool.

Lounge and soft seating

Upholstered chairs, benches and modular pieces from cafe corners and collaboration areas. These add comfort and break up rows of tables, but upholstery condition varies more than hard-shell seating, so grades matter.

Designer pieces

Corporate breakrooms often use seating from brands like Herman Miller, Steelcase, Knoll, Haworth, Allsteel, Kimball, Teknion and Friant. When those pieces come through a liquidation, you get a recognized commercial design at a pre-owned price. We sell them as an independent reseller, not an authorized dealer.

What to check before you buy

Whether you are looking at our inventory or anyone else's, these are the checks that separate a good used chair from a problem.

  • Frame welds and joints. Look for cracks where legs meet the seat frame. Stack chairs are dragged a lot, and this is where failures start.
  • Shell integrity. Plastic shells should flex slightly but show no stress cracks, especially around mounting points.
  • Glides and feet. Missing glides scratch floors and make chairs rock. They are inexpensive to replace, but count them in your budget.
  • Stool footrests and swivels. Footrests take a lot of force. Swivel stools should turn smoothly without grinding.
  • Upholstery. Check seams, foam firmness and stains on soft seating. Vinyl is easier to keep clean in food areas than fabric.
  • Matching. If you need 60 identical chairs, confirm the count, color and finish in writing before you schedule delivery.

Planning how many chairs you need

Start with how people actually use the room, not headcount alone. A breakroom that serves a staggered lunch rarely needs a seat for every employee. A cafeteria that also hosts company meetings may need more seats than tables.

  1. Count peak use. Estimate the largest group that will sit at once during a normal day.
  2. Match seats to tables. A standard 30-inch square table seats two to four. A 60-inch round seats about six. Plan tables and chairs together, and see our used breakroom tables for sizes.
  3. Add event overflow. If the room hosts all-hands meetings, stacking chairs give you extra seats that store in a corner.
  4. Mix heights. A counter with stools along a window uses space that tables cannot, and gives people a quick place to stand or perch.
  5. Leave circulation space. Keep clear walkways to the refrigerator, sink and exits. Crowded breakrooms feel smaller than they are.

Frequently asked questions

Where does your used breakroom seating come from?

It comes from corporate office liquidations and decommissions. When a company downsizes, relocates or closes a floor, the breakroom and cafeteria furniture is often removed along with the workstations. We inventory it, grade it and offer it for resale.

Can I get matching chairs for a large cafeteria?

Often, yes, because corporate cafeterias tend to buy one model in quantity. Availability depends on what is in stock at the time you ask. Send your target count and we will tell you what matching sets are available and whether mixing two compatible models makes sense.

Do manufacturer warranties come with used breakroom chairs?

Generally no. Office Station is an independent reseller, not an authorized dealer, and manufacturer warranties usually do not transfer to pre-owned furniture. That is why we grade every piece and describe its condition before you buy.

How much does delivery cost?

In Greater Phoenix, delivery is $195 in the central zone and $295 across the wider metro. Outside Greater Phoenix, freight is quoted per project based on quantity and destination.

What condition grade should I choose for a breakroom?

Breakroom seating takes more wear than almost anything else in an office, so many buyers choose Grade B for the best balance of price and appearance. Grade A makes sense for client-facing cafes. Grade C works well for warehouse break areas or training rooms where appearance matters less.
