Office Noise Reduction: Quieter Open Offices With Panels, Layout and Pre-Owned Furniture

Open offices get loud fast, but you rarely need a renovation to fix it. Panel height, desk orientation and a few well-placed pieces of acoustic furniture do most of the work.

6 minute read

Noise is one of the most common complaints in open offices. Conversations carry, phone calls interrupt focus, and hard surfaces bounce every sound around the room. The good news is that most office noise reduction comes from three levers you control: how tall your panels are, how your layout is zoned, and what the furniture is made of.

This guide walks through each lever, what it costs, and how to use pre-owned furniture to get results without paying new-furniture prices.

How noise travels in an open office

Sound reaches a listener in two main ways. It travels directly from a speaker's mouth across open air, and it reflects off ceilings, floors, windows and walls. A quiet office tackles both paths.

  • Block the direct path with panels, screens and distance.
  • Absorb reflected sound with fabric, upholstery and other soft surfaces.
  • Separate noisy activities from quiet ones through layout.

Most offices over-invest in one of these and ignore the others. Tall panels in a room with a bare concrete ceiling still sound loud, and a room full of soft furniture still suffers if the sales team sits beside accounting.

Choose panel heights by team, not by floor

Panel height is the biggest furniture decision for acoustics. Rather than picking one height for the whole office, match height to how each team works.

Panel approachWhat it doesBest for
Desktop screens on benchingBlocks visual distraction at the desk, little speech blockingShort-stay, collaborative or hybrid teams
Low panels, around seated eye levelSome direct-sound blocking while seated, open sightlinesTeams that talk to each other often
Tall panels, above seated head heightBlocks more direct speech between neighborsCall-heavy roles, finance, focus work
Tall panels with glass stackersHeight and blocking while letting daylight throughInterior rows where light is limited

If you already have a panel system, you may not need new stations. Stackers and replacement tiles can raise or change existing panels. That makes height a relatively low-cost adjustment compared with replacing the furniture.

Zone the layout before buying anything

Layout changes are often free and frequently deliver the most noticeable improvement. Walk the floor and sort activities into loud, medium and quiet.

  1. Map the noise sources. Printers, kitchens, entries, conference room doors and phone-heavy teams.
  2. Put buffers in between. Storage, filing and meeting rooms make good separators between loud and quiet zones.
  3. Avoid face-to-face seating for callers. People on calls project straight ahead. Back-to-back or staggered orientation keeps voices from aiming at a neighbor.
  4. Keep circulation paths out of focus zones. Foot traffic and hallway conversations follow the main aisles.
  5. Give calls a place to go. A few enclosed or semi-enclosed spots for calls take pressure off the open floor.

Use pre-owned acoustic furniture

Materials matter. Hard finishes reflect sound, and soft finishes absorb some of it. Upgrading surfaces in the right places helps the room feel calmer.

  • Fabric panels and tiles. Fabric-wrapped panel faces absorb more sound than laminate or glass. Swapping hard tiles for fabric on the sides facing the busiest aisles is a targeted fix.
  • Upholstered seating. Lounge chairs, booths and upholstered task chairs add soft surface area. See our pre-owned office chairs.
  • Screens on benching. Fabric desktop screens bring some separation to open benching without rebuilding it.
  • Storage as walls. Tall storage units create a physical barrier between zones and double as filing space.

Pre-owned systems from makers such as Steelcase, Haworth, Knoll, Herman Miller, Teknion and Allsteel regularly include these components. Quick office noise reduction checklist

  • Listed every loud activity and where it sits today
  • Moved call-heavy teams away from focus teams
  • Placed storage or meeting rooms as buffers
  • Matched panel height to each team's work style
  • Replaced hard tiles with fabric along busy aisles
  • Added screens to open benching
  • Created at least a few spots for private calls
  • Sold or removed surplus furniture that crowds the floor

That last item matters. Extra stations packed into a floor increase density and noise. If you are clearing surplus, an office furniture liquidation net offer can turn it into credit toward the pieces you actually need.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cheapest way to reduce noise in an open office?

Start with layout. Moving loud functions such as sales calls, printers and break areas away from focus seating costs little or nothing. After that, adding stackers or tiles to existing panels is usually cheaper than replacing furniture.

How tall should cubicle panels be to reduce noise?

Panels that rise above seated head height block more direct speech than low panels, which only block sightlines at the desk. Many offices use taller panels for focus and call-heavy teams and lower panels for collaborative teams. Height alone will not solve reflections off hard ceilings and floors, so combine it with soft surfaces.

Do fabric panels actually absorb sound?

Fabric-wrapped panels and tiles absorb some sound, while glass, laminate and metal reflect it. A mix is common: fabric where people talk and glass stackers where you want light. Ask about the panel construction when comparing systems.

Can I buy acoustic office furniture pre-owned?

Yes. Panel systems, fabric tiles, stackers and screens from brands like Steelcase, Haworth, Knoll and Herman Miller show up in pre-owned stock. Office Station sells them graded A, B or C as an independent reseller, and manufacturer warranties generally do not transfer.
