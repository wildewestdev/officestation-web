Cubicles vs Open Office: Privacy, Cost and Density Compared

The cubicles vs open office debate usually gets argued on feelings. This guide puts numbers on it, comparing privacy, per-seat cost and how many people each layout fits.

6 minute read

Few office decisions spark more debate than cubicles versus an open plan. One side hears "cubicle" and pictures a gray maze. The other hears "open office" and pictures a noisy room full of headphones. Both layouts can work well. The right answer depends on three measurable things: how much privacy the work requires, how much you can spend per seat and how many people must fit on the floor.

What we mean by each layout

Cubicles are panel-based workstations. Each person has a defined space bounded by panels, usually with attached work surfaces and storage. Panel heights range from low dividers to walls above standing height.

Open office layouts use benching or freestanding desks with little or no separation. People sit side by side or face to face along shared runs, often with low screens between positions.

In practice most offices land somewhere between, and that middle ground is often the best answer.

Privacy: cubicles win, by degrees

Privacy is where cubicles earn their reputation. Panels block sightlines, absorb some sound and give people a space that feels their own. The taller the panel, the more privacy, though taller panels also cut daylight and can make a floor feel closed.

Open offices trade privacy for visibility. That suits teams that need to talk constantly, but it creates problems for roles that handle confidential information, spend hours on the phone or need long stretches of concentration.

  • Choose cubicles for HR, finance, legal, support lines, analysts and supervisors.
  • Choose open seating for project teams, creative groups and rotating hybrid seats.
  • Consider mid-height panels as a compromise when a group does a bit of both.

Density: open plans fit more people

Density is the open office's strongest argument. A 6'x6' cubicle occupies 36 square feet before you add aisles. Benching positions are often narrower and share a central spine, which removes the panel and aisle space between rows. The same floor area typically seats more people in benching than in cubicles.

That matters when rent is your largest cost. But density has a ceiling. Pack people too tightly and noise, complaints and meeting-room demand rise until the savings erode. Your building, aisle widths, exits and local code also set hard limits, so test both layouts on your actual floor plan rather than relying on rules of thumb.

Switching layouts without wasting what you own

Changing from one layout to the other does not mean your current furniture is a loss. Office Station can evaluate the furniture you are replacing and provide a written net offer: its resale value minus the cost of removing it. If the number is positive, you are paid, and that money can go toward the new layout. Walkthroughs are typically within 48 hours in Greater Phoenix and 2 to 3 business days in other service markets, with offers within 5 business days.

Before you decide, work through these questions:

  1. Which roles need privacy for confidentiality or phone work?
  2. What is your peak in-office head count, not your total head count?
  3. What is your per-seat budget, and which tier fits it?
  4. When do you need to be moved in, and which tier's lead time meets that date?
  5. What will you do with the furniture you are replacing?

Frequently asked questions

Are cubicles more expensive than an open office?

Per seat, cubicles usually cost more because they use more panels and components than open benching. The gap narrows when you buy pre-owned. A 6'x6' workstation, furniture only, is $615 liquidation-grade, $1,245 remanufactured or $1,920 new, excluding electrical, delivery and installation.

How many people fit in a cubicle layout versus an open plan?

A 6'x6' cubicle occupies 36 square feet before aisles, while open benching positions can be narrower and share a spine, which usually fits more people in the same area. Actual density depends on your building, aisle widths and local code, so use free space planning to test both layouts on your floor plan.

Which layout is better for productivity?

It depends on the work. Focus-heavy, confidential and phone-heavy roles tend to do better with cubicle panels, while teams that collaborate constantly often prefer open seating. Many offices mix both, with quiet cubicle zones and open collaboration zones.

Can I switch from an open office to cubicles without buying everything new?

Yes. Pre-owned cubicles are available at liquidation-grade and remanufactured tiers, and surplus benching you no longer need can be evaluated for a written net offer. That offer is the resale value minus removal cost, and a positive number means you are paid.
