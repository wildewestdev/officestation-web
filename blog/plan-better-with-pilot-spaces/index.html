Plan Better With an Office Pilot Space

A pilot space lets real employees work in a proposed layout before you commit to it. Built with low-cost liquidation-grade workstations, it turns a design debate into evidence.

6 minute read

Floor plans look convincing on a screen. Then people move in and discover that the collaboration zone is too loud, the quiet rows are empty and nobody uses the lockers. By then the budget is spent. An office pilot space fixes that sequence: you test the idea at small scale, learn what works and scale only the parts that earned it.

Why pilot before you build

A full office buildout commits you to a layout for years. A pilot costs a fraction of that and answers questions a floor plan cannot.

  • Density. Does a 6'x6' footprint feel right, or do people need more surface or more separation?
  • Panel height. Do low panels help teams talk, or do they drive people into conference rooms to concentrate?
  • Mix. What ratio of assigned desks, shared desks and meeting space does a team really use?
  • Storage. How much do people keep at their desk when given the choice?
  • Adoption. Will employees choose the space if they have an alternative?

How to set up a pilot space

  1. Write the questions first. List the two or three decisions the pilot must inform, such as seat ratio or panel height. If a question will not change a decision, drop it.
  2. Pick a representative team. Choose a group whose work mix resembles most of the company. Avoid teams that are unusually remote or unusually heads-down.
  3. Choose a contained area. Use a zone near power and data so electrical work stays simple. Corners or a single bay work well.
  4. Plan the layout. Take advantage of free space planning to draw two options if you can, such as one row of low panels and one of higher panels.
  5. Order and install. Installation is $95 per workstation with a $495 minimum. Schedule your electrician for power connections, since that is not included.
  6. Set a start and end date. Six to eight weeks is a practical window.
  7. Communicate clearly. Tell the team this is a test, that their feedback shapes the final design and that changes are expected.
Build in one variable, not five. If you change panel height, seating, storage and seat assignment all at once, you will not know which one caused the result. Change one or two things per zone so the feedback points to a clear decision.

What to measure during the pilot

Opinions matter, but pair them with observation so the loudest voice does not set the design.

  • Daily headcount in the pilot area at a few fixed times.
  • Which seats fill first and which stay empty.
  • Conference room bookings by the pilot team, before and during the test.
  • A short survey at week two and again at the end, using the same questions.
  • Noise complaints and requests to move.
  • Storage use: full drawers, empty pedestals, piles on surfaces.

Turning results into a buildout decision

At the end of the pilot, compare what you measured against the questions you wrote at the start. Most pilots land in one of three places.

  • The layout works. Roll it out. You can keep the pilot stations in place and match them in the rest of the floor, or upgrade to pre-owned designer furniture for the permanent build.
  • It partly works. Adjust the variable that underperformed and run a shorter second round.
  • It does not work. That is still a win. You avoided buying a full floor of the wrong furniture, and the pilot stations can move to a training room, project room or overflow area.

Frequently asked questions

What is an office pilot space?

An office pilot space is a small, working section of your office set up in a proposed layout. A team uses it for several weeks while you gather feedback and usage data. The results guide the full buildout.

How big should a pilot space be?

Big enough for one real team to do its normal work, often 10 to 20 seats. Too small and you only test furniture. Too large and the pilot becomes an expensive partial buildout.

How much does a pilot space cost to furnish?

Using liquidation-grade 6'x6' workstations at $615 each, 12 stations cost $7,380 in furniture. Installation is $95 per workstation with a $495 minimum, and delivery and electrical are separate.

What happens to the pilot furniture afterward?

You can keep it as part of the permanent space, move it to overflow or project areas, or reconfigure it. If the pilot proves a different approach, the workstations can still serve a training room or satellite team.

How long should a pilot run?

Plan on six to eight weeks. The first couple of weeks reflect novelty and adjustment, so the later weeks give you a more honest read on how people actually work in the space.
