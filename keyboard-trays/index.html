Ergonomic Keyboard Trays: When They Fix the Desk You Already Have

A keyboard tray can solve a desk that is too high for the person using it, often for far less than a new worksurface. This guide shows when a tray is the right fix, when it is not, and how to add one to pre-owned workstations.

6 minute read

Here is a problem that shows up in almost every office: the desk is the same height for everyone, but people are not. A shorter employee raises their chair to reach the keyboard, their feet leave the floor, and their shoulders creep up toward their ears by mid-afternoon.

Ergonomic keyboard trays exist for exactly this. They lower the keyboard and mouse below the worksurface so the typing height matches the person instead of the furniture. Used well, they extend the life of the desks you already own.

When a keyboard tray is the right fix

A tray solves a height mismatch between the user and a fixed desk. It is worth considering when you see these signs:

  • Elbows sit below the desk edge when the person sits with feet flat
  • Wrists rest on a hard desk edge while typing
  • Shoulders are raised or hunched during keyboard work
  • The chair is raised so high that feet dangle or need a footrest
  • The mouse sits far to the side, forcing a long reach

The target posture is simple. Sit with feet flat, thighs roughly level, and upper arms hanging relaxed. Bend your elbows to about 90 degrees. Your keyboard should be at or slightly below that elbow height. If the desk surface is higher than that point, a tray brings the keys down to meet you.

When a tray will not help much

  • The desk is already too low. Trays that go above the surface exist, but raising the worksurface or choosing a height-adjustable table is usually a better fix.
  • The problem is the chair. A chair without adjustable seat height, depth or lumbar support will cause discomfort no matter where the keyboard sits. Start with a proper task chair.
  • The monitor is wrong. Neck strain usually comes from screen height and distance, which a tray does not change.
  • Laptop-only work. Lowering a laptop drops the screen too. Pair a tray with an external monitor or laptop stand.
Fix the chair first. Set seat height for feet flat on the floor, then measure elbow height. Only then decide whether the desk needs a tray. Doing it in the other order leads to buying trays that fix nothing.

How to measure for a keyboard tray

Take these measurements before shopping. Five minutes here prevents most returns.

  1. Seated elbow height. With the chair adjusted correctly, measure from the floor to the underside of the relaxed forearm.
  2. Worksurface height. Floor to the top of the desk. The gap between this and elbow height is roughly how far the tray must drop.
  3. Surface thickness. Most trays hang below the surface, so the mechanism takes up some of that drop.
  4. Clear depth underneath. Measure from the front edge back to the first obstruction, such as a panel, modesty screen or support bracket. The track must fit in that space.
  5. Knee clearance. With the tray lowered, the user's thighs still need room under it. Tall users with deep trays can run out of space.
  6. Pedestal and leg locations. A fixed pedestal can block the tray from sliding or swiveling to the side.

Adding trays to pre-owned workstations

Keyboard trays pair naturally with pre-owned furniture. If you buy used or remanufactured workstations, the worksurfaces are typically standard fixed heights. Adding trays at the stations that need them is a targeted fix that keeps the savings intact.

For reference, a 6'x6' workstation is $615 liquidation-grade, $1,245 remanufactured or $1,920 new, furniture only. Swapping an entire floor to adjustable-height tables costs far more than equipping only the users who need a lower keyboard.

What to check on a used worksurface

  • Underside is solid core, not damaged or delaminated where screws will go
  • No existing holes in the exact spot the new track needs to mount
  • Support brackets and cantilevers leave a clear run for the track
  • Surface is deep enough, since shallow corner or return surfaces may not fit a long track
  • Condition grade matches expectations. Office Station grades pre-owned pieces A like new, B very good and C good.

Mention trays when you plan your layout. Free space planning can place pedestals and supports so tray tracks fit at the stations that need them, rather than discovering a conflict on install day.

Installation notes

Tray tracks mount with screws into the underside of the surface, which is easiest before a workstation is fully loaded with monitors and equipment. A simple rollout plan for an office

  1. Ask employees to report discomfort at the desk, not just request equipment.
  2. Adjust chairs first and re-check elbow height at each reported station.
  3. Install trays where the desk sits above elbow height after chair adjustment.
  4. Choose height-adjustable trays for any shared or hot-desk station.
  5. Re-check a few weeks later and adjust monitor height to match the new posture.

Frequently asked questions

Do ergonomic keyboard trays actually help?

They help when the fixed desk height is too high for the user, which is common because many worksurfaces sit near 29 to 30 inches. A tray drops the keyboard and mouse so elbows can stay near 90 degrees with relaxed shoulders. If the desk is already at the right height, a tray adds little.

What should I look for in a keyboard tray?

Look for height adjustment below and above the desk surface, negative tilt, a platform wide enough for both keyboard and mouse, and a mechanism that locks firmly without wobble. Check the track length against the depth of your worksurface before buying.

Can I add a keyboard tray to a used cubicle?

Usually yes. Most trays mount to a track screwed into the underside of the worksurface, so they work with most panel system and freestanding desks. You need enough clear depth under the surface and no pedestal, modesty panel or support blocking the track.

Is a keyboard tray better than a height-adjustable desk?

It depends on the goal. A tray fixes keying height for seated work at a lower cost, while a height-adjustable desk also allows standing and moves the monitor with the surface. Many offices use trays on existing stations and reserve sit-stand desks for people who need them.
