Open Plan Office Space: A Practical Planning Guide

Open plan office space can boost collaboration and fit more people per square foot, or it can turn into a noisy room nobody wants to work in. This guide covers layout choices, benching versus workstations, and what each option costs.

7 minute read

What open plan office space really means

An open plan office replaces private offices and tall cubicles with shared space: rows of desks or low-panel workstations, with meeting and focus rooms carved out around them. The goal is easier communication, more daylight across the floor and better use of every square foot.

The risk is equally clear. Without enough privacy and quiet space, people put on headphones, book every conference room for solo work, or stay home. A successful open plan is not the absence of walls. It is a deliberate mix of open and enclosed space.

When open plan works, and when it does not

Open plan tends to work well for

  • Teams that collaborate constantly, such as sales, customer success, product and creative groups
  • Companies growing fast that need to add seats without rebuilding walls
  • Hybrid offices where attendance varies and desks can be shared
  • Floors with good daylight that tall panels would block

Be cautious with open plan for

  • Roles that handle confidential calls or documents, such as HR, legal and finance
  • Work that needs long stretches of deep concentration
  • Teams that spend most of the day on the phone

Most offices land on a hybrid: an open core for collaborative teams, lower-panel workstations for focused roles, and enclosed rooms for everything that needs a door.

Layout principles for a better open plan

Zone by noise, not by org chart

Put the phone-heavy teams together and away from focus-heavy teams. Place kitchens, printers and entries at the loud end of the floor.

Give every open seat a nearby escape

Plan small phone rooms and focus rooms close to open areas. If people have to walk across the building to take a call, they will take it at their desk instead.

Use panel height on purpose

Low panels between facing desks block direct eye contact without blocking light. Taller panels along main aisles cut the distraction of foot traffic.

Plan power before furniture

Without walls, power has to come from the floor, the ceiling or the furniture itself. Decide early, because it affects which benching or workstation systems you can use.

Protect circulation

Keep main aisles clear and wide enough for egress. Dense benching runs can creep into walkways if nobody checks the final dimensions.

Size to your busiest day. In a hybrid office, count how many people are in on your peak day, not your average. Buy seats for that number plus some margin, and you avoid both empty rows and people with nowhere to sit.

Open plan planning checklist

  1. Map which teams collaborate and which need quiet.
  2. Count peak-day attendance and projected growth.
  3. Decide on assigned, shared or bookable seating.
  4. Choose benching, workstations or a mix for each zone.
  5. Set a ratio of enclosed rooms to open seats.
  6. Confirm power and data routes with your electrician.
  7. Pick tiers by zone, matching lead time to your move date.
  8. Check aisle widths and egress with your building.
  9. Plan storage: lockers, pedestals or shared files.

Frequently asked questions

Is benching cheaper than cubicles for an open plan office?

Benching usually fits more people in the same area because it uses shared worksurfaces and little or no panel walling. Whether it costs less per person depends on the product and tier. Compare benching against the workstation tiers, where a 6'x6' station runs $615 liquidation-grade, $1,245 remanufactured or $1,920 new, furniture only.

How do you control noise in an open plan office?

Mix in low panels or screens between facing desks, group loud and quiet teams apart, and add small enclosed rooms for calls. Soft surfaces such as fabric panels, rugs and acoustic ceiling treatment also help absorb sound.

Can I use pre-owned furniture in an open plan layout?

Yes. Pre-owned workstations and benching work well in open plans, especially when a whole matching lot comes from one floor. Office Station grades pieces A like new, B very good or C good, and we sell as an independent reseller, so manufacturer warranties generally do not transfer.

How long does it take to furnish an open plan office?

It depends on the tier. Liquidation-grade stock can be ready in as little as 72 hours, remanufactured usually takes 7 to 14 days, and new takes 4 to 6 weeks. Installation is $95 per workstation with a $495 minimum.

Should every employee get an assigned desk?

Not always. If attendance is well under full headcount most days, shared or bookable desks can reduce the number of stations you buy. Keep some assigned seats for people in the office daily, and plan enough capacity for your busiest day.
