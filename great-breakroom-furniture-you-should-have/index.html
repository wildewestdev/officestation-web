Breakroom guide

Breakroom Furniture You Should Have

A breakroom works when people can eat, refill and reset without bumping into each other. Use this checklist to furnish yours, with pre-owned options that keep the budget reasonable.

5 minute read

Breakrooms get less design attention than lobbies and conference rooms, yet almost everyone in the office uses them every day. A cramped or awkward breakroom sends people back to eat at their desks. A good one gives them a real break and a place to run into coworkers.

This checklist focuses on the furniture itself: what to include, what to look for, and how to source it without overspending.

Start with zones, not furniture

Before picking pieces, decide what the room needs to do. Most breakrooms have four zones:

  1. Prep and refill. Counter, sink, coffee, microwave, refrigerator.
  2. Eating. Tables and chairs where people sit for a full meal.
  3. Quick stop. A high-top or ledge for a coffee and a two-minute conversation.
  4. Unwind. Soft seating for people who want a real break from their desk posture.

Keep the prep zone from spilling into the eating zone. The most common breakroom complaint is a line at the microwave blocking the path to the tables.

Plan for the peak, not the headcount. A 60-person office may only have 15 people in the breakroom at once. Count your busiest lunch window before you order chairs, and put the savings into better tables or a lounge corner.

Breakroom furniture ideas by office type

Small office with one shared room

Choose one or two mid-size tables that can join for a team lunch, a counter ledge along a window or wall, and a single storage cabinet. Skip the lounge set if space is tight and use a comfortable bench instead.

Hybrid office with variable attendance

Go flexible. Light chairs and tables that can be reconfigured work better than fixed booths when attendance swings day to day. A few lockers help people who do not have an assigned desk.

Larger floor with a true cafe space

Mix seating heights: standard dining tables, a high-top row and a lounge corner. That spreads people across the room and gives quieter options to those who want them. Consider a second, smaller coffee point elsewhere on the floor to take pressure off the main room.

Leave room to move

Furniture that fits on paper can still fail in use. Chairs pushed back from a table need space behind them, and people carrying hot drinks need a clear path that does not cut through seated diners. Keep the route from the door to the refrigerator and trash open, and do not park tables in front of cabinet doors or the appliance line.

If anyone on your team uses a wheelchair or mobility aid, plan at least one table height and approach path that works for them, and keep lockers and supplies within easy reach.

Sourcing breakroom furniture pre-owned

Breakroom furniture takes a beating from spills, dragged chairs and daily cleaning. That makes durable commercial pieces a better investment than light residential furniture, and it makes pre-owned a sensible way to get them.

Frequently asked questions

What furniture does every breakroom need?

At minimum, a breakroom needs durable tables, seating that is easy to clean, counter or cabinet storage, and a clear place for trash and recycling. Beyond that, add a lounge corner or a high-top counter based on how your team actually uses the room.

How many seats should a breakroom have?

It depends on how many people take breaks at the same time, not your total headcount. Watch the busiest lunch window for a week and plan seating for that peak with a little extra. Free space planning can help you fit that count without crowding walkways.

Is pre-owned furniture a good choice for a breakroom?

Breakrooms see spills and heavy traffic, so durable commercial furniture matters more than showroom polish. Pre-owned tables and chairs in Grade B or Grade C condition can be a practical, lower-cost fit. Choose surfaces and fabrics that clean easily.
