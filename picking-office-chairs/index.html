How to Choose an Office Chair

The right chair fits the person using it and the work they do. This guide covers the adjustments that matter, how to test fit, and what to know before buying pre-owned.

7 minute read

Most people choose an office chair by sitting in it for ten seconds. That tells you whether it feels soft, not whether it will support you through an eight-hour day. A better approach is to know which adjustments matter, fit the chair to the actual user and understand what you are getting if you buy pre-owned.

Start with the person and the work

Before you look at a single chair, answer three questions:

  • Who sits in it? One person all day, or several people across shifts or hoteling desks? Shared chairs need a wider adjustment range and easy controls.
  • How long do they sit? A full-time task chair deserves more investment than a chair used for a one-hour meeting.
  • What is the work? Heavy keyboard use, frequent reaching, call center work and drafting at a tall surface each call for different features.

The adjustments that matter most

These are listed roughly in order of importance for a typical desk worker.

Seat height

Feet should rest flat on the floor with thighs roughly level and knees near a right angle. If the desk is too high to allow that, you may need a footrest or a height-adjustable surface. Check that the chair's height range suits both your shortest and tallest users.

Seat depth

When you sit back against the backrest, you should have a gap of about two to four finger widths between the front edge of the seat and the back of your knees. Too deep, and shorter users perch forward without back support. Too shallow, and taller users lose thigh support. A sliding seat pan solves this for mixed users.

Lumbar support

The backrest should meet the natural curve of your lower back. Adjustable-height or adjustable-depth lumbar lets you place support where it belongs. Some backrests flex to provide support without a separate adjustment.

Recline and tilt tension

Leaning back slightly shifts load off the spine. A good chair lets you recline smoothly and set tension to match your body weight, so you are not fighting the chair or falling backward. A recline lock at a few positions adds control.

Armrests

Arms should let your shoulders relax with elbows near your sides. Height adjustment is the most important arm feature. Width and pivot adjustment help for keyboard work. If arms cannot go low enough to slide under the desk, they push you away from your work.

Base and casters

A five-star base is standard for stability. Match casters to the floor: harder casters for carpet, softer ones for hard floors.

A five-minute fit test

  1. Set seat height so feet are flat and thighs are level.
  2. Sit fully back and check the gap behind your knees.
  3. Move the lumbar support until it fills the curve of your lower back.
  4. Adjust tilt tension, then lean back and forward a few times.
  5. Lower the arms until shoulders relax, then pull up to your desk.
  6. Type for a few minutes. Note any pressure points on thighs, tailbone or shoulders.

If a chair cannot pass this test after adjustment, it does not fit that person, no matter the brand or price.

What to inspect on a used chair

  • Every lever and knob works and holds its setting.
  • The gas cylinder holds height without slowly sinking.
  • Recline is smooth, with no clunking or grinding.
  • Seat foam or mesh is supportive, with no sagging, tears or fraying.
  • Arm pads are intact and arms do not wobble.
  • All casters roll freely and the base has no cracks.

Warranty realities

This is the part many buyers do not expect. Office Station is an independent reseller, not an authorized dealer for the brands we carry. Manufacturer warranties generally do not transfer to pre-owned furniture. That does not make a used chair a bad purchase, but it does change how you should buy:

  • Rely on the condition grade and your own inspection, not a brand's warranty terms.
  • Ask for photos of the mechanism, cylinder and seat before ordering.
  • Consider the savings against the cost of replacing parts like cylinders or arm pads over time.
Do not pay for a warranty you will not get. If a listing for a used chair promises a full manufacturer warranty, ask how that transfer works in writing. For pre-owned seating, it generally does not, so compare prices on condition and function alone.

Choosing chairs for a whole office

For a team, standardizing on one or two models simplifies maintenance and keeps the floor looking consistent. Pick models with broad adjustment ranges, plan a handful of alternates for very tall, petite or larger users, and have a few people test samples before committing. After delivery, spend ten minutes showing each person how to adjust their chair. Unadjusted chairs are one of the most common reasons good seating still gets complaints.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most important feature in an office chair?

Adjustability that matches the user. Seat height, seat depth and lumbar support usually matter most, because they set how the hips, thighs and lower back are supported. A chair with many features that does not fit the person is still the wrong chair.

Is a pre-owned designer chair better than a new budget chair?

Often, yes. Commercial task chairs from major manufacturers are built for years of daily use, and a well-kept pre-owned one can offer more adjustment and durability than an inexpensive new chair. Check the condition grade and test every mechanism.

Do warranties come with pre-owned office chairs?

Generally no. Office Station is an independent reseller, not an authorized dealer, and manufacturer warranties usually do not transfer to pre-owned furniture. Factor that into your decision and inspect the chair carefully.

How do I choose chairs for a whole team?

Pick a chair with a wide range of adjustment so it fits most body sizes, then plan a few alternates for people outside that range. Have several employees test sample chairs before you order in volume, and show everyone how to adjust their chair.

What condition grade should I buy?

Grade A is like new, B is very good and C is good. For daily task chairs, A or B is a common choice. Grade C can make sense for training rooms, overflow seating or budgets where function matters more than appearance.
