How to Create Privacy in an Open Office

Privacy in a shared office comes in layers: sight lines, sound and personal territory. Here is how to add each one, from simple panel changes to full cubicles, and what it costs per station.

6 minute read

Open offices were supposed to spark collaboration. For many teams, they also created constant interruptions, overheard calls and the feeling of being watched. If you are figuring out how to create privacy in an open office, the good news is that you rarely need a renovation. You need the right mix of layout, panel height and furniture, matched to how each group actually works.

The three kinds of privacy you are solving for

Before you buy anything, name the problem. Most complaints fall into one of three buckets, and each has a different fix.

  • Visual privacy. People feel exposed when a screen faces an aisle or when they sit directly across from a colleague. Blocking sight lines at seated eye level solves most of this.
  • Acoustic privacy. Voices carry across open floors. Fabric panels, distance and separate places for calls matter more here than walls alone.
  • Territorial privacy. Some people simply want a defined space that is theirs, with storage they can lock. This is about boundaries, not height.

Survey your team or walk the floor at a busy hour. You may find that one department needs acoustic relief while another just needs desks turned away from the break room.

Start with layout, which is free

Rearranging what you already have is the fastest privacy upgrade.

  • Turn monitors so they face a wall or panel, not the main walkway.
  • Avoid face-to-face desk rows for roles that spend the day on calls.
  • Move high-traffic points like printers, coffee and entries away from focus seating.
  • Use storage cabinets and tall files as dividers between teams.
  • Separate loud and quiet groups by distance rather than by walls.

Choosing panel heights

Panel height is the main dial on a workstation system. Exact heights vary by manufacturer and product line, so treat the ranges below as general bands rather than specifications.

Panel height bandWhat it blocksBest forTradeoff
Low, around desk heightClutter and leg areas onlyCollaborative teams, benchingLittle visual or sound privacy
Mid, near seated eye levelSeated sight lines to neighborsMost general office rolesStanding people can still see over
Tall, above seated head heightSeated views and more voice spillHeavy phone work, focused tasksLess daylight and casual contact
Full height with or without glass topsMost views, including standingHR, finance, confidential rolesFeels closed in without glass or good lighting

A common approach is to keep lower panels on the aisle side, so the floor still feels open, and taller panels between neighbors, where privacy matters most. Glass or translucent stackers on top of a panel let light through while still breaking up sight lines.

Mind your electrical before you raise panels. Powered panels carry wiring through the base. Changing heights or layouts can mean a new electrical plan, and that cost is separate from the furniture. Get your electrician involved before you finalize the layout.

When to move from benching to cubicles

If panels on existing desks are not enough, full workstations are the next step. Cubicles give each person a defined footprint, a work surface sized for their job and a place for storage. They are the most direct way to address all three kinds of privacy at once.

Cubicles make sense when:

  • Most of the day is spent on calls or video meetings.
  • People handle confidential information on screen or on paper.
  • Complaints about noise and interruptions keep coming back after layout fixes.
  • Your team wants assigned seats rather than hot desks.

Benching still has a place for teams that work together all day. Many offices end up with both, and that is usually cheaper than converting the whole floor.

Add a few places to escape

Even the best workstation will not host a sensitive conversation. Plan for a small number of enclosed spaces that anyone can grab: a converted office, a small meeting room or a corner with full-height partitions. Set simple norms, such as taking calls longer than a few minutes to one of these spaces. Pair that with higher panels at desks and most offices see a real change.

A quick privacy planning checklist

  1. List which teams need visual, acoustic or territorial privacy.
  2. Fix sight lines and traffic paths with the furniture you already own.
  3. Decide panel heights by role, not one height for the whole floor.
  4. Check electrical and data needs before changing panel layouts.
  5. Choose a condition tier for each zone based on budget and visibility.
  6. Get a space plan to confirm station counts and aisle widths.
  7. Designate a few enclosed rooms for calls and private conversations.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cheapest way to add privacy to an open office?

Start with layout. Turning desks so people do not face each other or a busy aisle costs nothing. After that, adding or raising panels on existing workstations is usually cheaper than replacing furniture. Liquidation-grade workstations are the lowest-cost way to add full cubicles.

What panel height gives the most privacy?

Panels that reach above seated eye level block sight lines for people sitting down, and full-height panels block standing views as well. Taller panels also absorb more sound when they have fabric faces. The tradeoff is less daylight and less casual collaboration, so many offices mix heights by role.

How much does a cubicle cost?

At Office Station, a 6'x6' workstation, furniture only, is $615 liquidation-grade, $1,245 remanufactured and $1,920 new. Prices exclude electrical, delivery and professional installation. Volume pricing starts at 20 stations.

Do cubicles solve noise problems?

They help, especially with fabric panels at or above seated head height, but they do not make a space quiet on their own. Pair them with a few enclosed rooms for calls and a clear policy about where meetings happen. Hard ceilings and floors will still reflect sound.

Can I mix cubicles and open desks in one office?

Yes, and it is often the best answer. Put focused and confidential roles in higher-panel stations and keep collaborative teams on lower panels or benching. A free space plan can show how the two zones fit on your floor.
