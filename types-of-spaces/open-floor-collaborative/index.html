Open Office Collaborative Layout: A Practical Planning Guide

An open plan lives or dies on how the stations are arranged. This guide compares benching and workstation layouts, walks through the math, and shows what each station really costs.

7 minute read

An open office collaborative layout is supposed to make it easy for people to talk, share and move. Done poorly, it becomes a noisy room full of desks where nobody can concentrate. The difference usually comes down to three decisions: which furniture system you use, how you cluster it, and where you put the spaces between clusters.

This guide covers all three, then shows what an open floor costs at each of Office Station's price tiers.

Benching vs. workstations

Most open plans are built from one of two systems, or a blend of both.

Benching

Benching runs a shared frame down a line, with people seated side by side and often facing another row. Dividers, if any, are low. It fits more people into less space and makes quick conversation effortless. The tradeoffs are less personal storage, less visual privacy and more noise travel.

Low-panel workstations

Workstations such as a 6'x6' layout give each person a defined footprint, panels that can be kept low for sightlines, and room for storage. They take more floor area per person, but they reduce distraction and give teams a sense of ownership over their space.

Hybrid plans

Many offices use workstations for roles that need focus and benching for teams that rotate, travel or collaborate constantly. Mixing systems in one plan works well as long as finishes and heights are coordinated.

Four layout patterns that work

  1. Team pods. Clusters of four to eight stations grouped by department, with a shared table or whiteboard at the end of each pod. This is the most common collaborative pattern and the easiest to reconfigure.
  2. Spine and neighborhood. A main aisle runs through the floor, with team neighborhoods branching off. The spine carries traffic and noise so neighborhoods stay calmer.
  3. Perimeter focus, core collaboration. Workstations sit along windows for daylight and quiet, while meeting tables, lounge seating and huddle areas fill the interior.
  4. Buffered zones. Storage, planters or taller panels separate loud activity from quiet work. A lounge placed between two team areas can act as both a buffer and a shared hub. See our office lounge furniture page for seating ideas.
Plan the in-between space first. Open plans fail most often because every square foot goes to desks. Before counting stations, block out circulation aisles, a few enclosed rooms for calls, and at least one shared collaboration zone. Then fill the remaining area with stations.

Choosing a tier for each zone

You do not have to pick one tier for the whole floor. Many open plans mix them:

  • Liquidation-grade for a fast opening, a temporary floor or a growing team that needs seats this week.
  • Remanufactured for the main workforce, where you want a consistent, refreshed look at a mid-range price.
  • New for areas where a specific finish or configuration is required and you can wait 4 to 6 weeks.

Open plan planning checklist

  • Headcount today and expected growth over your lease term
  • Assigned seating, hot desking or a mix
  • Floor plan with column locations, windows and existing power
  • Main aisle and emergency egress widths confirmed with your building
  • Number of enclosed rooms for calls and private meetings
  • Panel heights by zone, lower for teams and taller for focus areas
  • Storage needs per person
  • Delivery access, freight elevator and dock availability

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between benching and workstations?

Benching is a shared-frame run of work surfaces with people sitting side by side, usually with low or no dividers. Workstations, like 6'x6' cubicles, give each person a defined footprint with panels and often more storage. Benching packs more people per square foot, while workstations give more privacy and acoustic separation.

How much does a 6'x6' workstation cost?

Furniture-only pricing is $615 for liquidation-grade, $1,245 for remanufactured and $1,920 for new. Those prices exclude electrical, delivery and professional installation. Installation is $95 per workstation with a $495 minimum.

How fast can I get stations for an open floor?
