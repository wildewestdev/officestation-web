Office Lounge Furniture That Gets Used

A lounge is where people take calls, wait for meetings and reset between tasks. Here is how to furnish one with pre-owned designer seating, what the condition grades mean, and what to check before you buy.

6 minute read

Office lounge furniture has a harder job than it looks. A single sofa might host a client waiting for a meeting at 9, a two-person huddle at 11 and someone eating lunch at 1. It has to look sharp, hold up to constant use and still feel comfortable enough that people actually sit in it.

Buying pre-owned lets you put designer-level seating in that space without paying new-furniture prices. Office Station sells pre-owned furniture from brands like Herman Miller, Steelcase, Knoll, Haworth, Teknion, Allsteel, Kimball, AIS and Friant, graded so you know what you are getting.

What an office lounge actually needs

Before picking pieces, decide what the lounge is for. Most fall into one of three patterns, and each one changes the furniture list.

Social or break lounge

People gather, talk and eat. You want a mix of sofas and lounge chairs arranged in conversational clusters, plus low or occasional tables sized for a coffee cup and a plate. Durable, cleanable surfaces matter more here than anywhere else.

Touchdown or work lounge

People bring laptops and stay a while. Look for lounge chairs with tablet arms or side tables at laptop height, and plan for power near every seat. Upright seat angles work better than deep, reclined ones.

Semi-private retreat

People step away to take a call or focus. High-back lounge chairs and sofas with wraparound panels create a sense of enclosure without building walls. Spread these out rather than clustering them.

How condition grades work for lounge seating

Every pre-owned piece we sell carries a condition grade. Lounge furniture shows wear differently than a desk or a task chair, so here is how to read the grades in this category.

  • Grade A, like new. Upholstery and frames show little or no visible wear. A good fit for client-facing lounges and executive areas where appearance is the priority.
  • Grade B, very good. Light wear you might notice up close, such as minor scuffs on legs or slight softening of cushions. A strong value for employee lounges and touchdown spaces.
  • Grade C, good. Visible signs of use, but sound structure and full function. Works well for break rooms, overflow seating and budget-first projects.

Mixing grades is a practical move. Put Grade A pieces where visitors see them first and Grade B or C pieces in back-of-house areas.

Pre-owned lounge furniture buying checklist

Use this list when you review pieces, whether you are looking at photos or seeing them in person.

  1. Sit in it, or ask someone to. Cushion foam loses resilience over time. Ask whether the seat springs back or holds an impression.
  2. Check upholstery type. Leather and coated fabrics wipe clean. Woven fabrics feel warmer but show stains. Match the material to how the space will be used.
  3. Confirm dimensions against your plan. Lounge pieces are deep. A sofa that fits the wall can still block a walkway once people stretch their legs.
  4. Look at the base. Loose legs, missing glides and cracked welds are the most common structural issues.
  5. Ask about matching sets. If you need four identical chairs, confirm quantity and fabric match before you plan around them.
  6. Plan for power. If the lounge is a work area, know where outlets are and whether you need tables with built-in power.
  7. Budget delivery. Greater Phoenix delivery is $195 central or $295 metro. Freight elsewhere is quoted per project.

Planning a lounge layout

Good lounges share a few layout habits. Keep main circulation paths clear so people cross the space without weaving between chairs. Group seating in clusters of two to four so conversations feel natural. Put the most enclosed seating farthest from the entry and the most open seating closest to it.

Think about noise, too. A lounge next to a row of workstations will pull conversation into focus space.
