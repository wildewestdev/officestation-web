Flip-top and nesting tables

Training Room Tables

Training rooms change shape more than any other space in the office. The right tables fold, nest and roll so one room can host a lecture on Monday and breakout groups on Tuesday.

6 minute read

A training room table has a different job than a desk. It gets moved, stored, linked and reset constantly, often by whoever booked the room that day. Buying for that reality, rather than for the room photo, is what keeps a training space useful for years.

Flip-top versus nesting tables

Almost every modern training room table is one of these two styles. The difference shows up when the room needs to be cleared.

FeatureFlip-topNestingFixed-leg
Top folds to verticalYesYesNo
Stores tightly in a rowPartiallyYes, legs interlockNo
CastersUsuallyAlmost alwaysSometimes
Reconfiguration speedFastFastestSlow
Stability at restGoodGood with locking castersBest
Best forRooms reset weeklyMulti-use rooms cleared oftenPermanent classroom rows

If your room doubles as an event space, a lunch room or an all-hands area, nesting tables pay for themselves in reset time. If the layout stays in classroom rows for months at a time, a simpler flip-top or even a fixed-leg table can be the better value.

Specs that matter

Size

Pick one standard size for the whole room. Tables of the same width and depth combine into rows, U-shapes, squares and pods without gaps. Two people side by side need at least 60 inches of width. For laptop work, a 30 inch depth keeps screens and notes from crowding each other.

Base and casters

Look for locking casters on every leg, not just two. A table that drifts while someone writes is the most common complaint in training rooms. T-leg bases give more knee room than four-leg designs, which helps when people sit at the ends.

Modesty panels

A front panel adds privacy in lecture layouts and gives a mounting point for cable management. It can also block knees when tables are pushed together face to face, so think about which layouts you use most.

Power and data

Laptops in a full-day session need power. Options include flip-up power modules in the top, grommets feeding floor boxes, and cords that daisy-chain between linked tables. Confirm how power reaches the room and whether your layout crosses walkways. Electrical work is not included in furniture pricing.

Linking hardware

Connectors that clip tables together keep rows straight and stop gaps from opening mid-session. They are a small add-on that makes a big difference in how the room feels.

Count storage space, not just floor space. Before you order, measure where the tables will live when the room is cleared. A room of nested tables still needs a wall or a closet deep enough to hold them, plus a clear path to roll them there.

Planning a full room

Outfitting a whole training room at once is simpler and more consistent than buying in batches. Use this checklist to build your order.

  • Maximum seated capacity: how many people in your largest session.
  • Primary layout: classroom rows, U-shape, pods or boardroom.
  • Secondary layouts: what else the room gets used for.
  • Table size: one standard width and depth.
  • Presenter station: a lectern or a separate table at the front.
  • Power: modules, floor boxes or wall outlets only.
  • Seating: stackable or nesting chairs that match the table height.
  • Storage: where nested tables and stacked chairs will go.
  • Timeline: the date the first session is booked.

As a quick guide, classroom rows of 60 inch tables seat two per table, so a 24 seat classroom takes about 12 tables plus a presenter table. U-shapes and pods use more tables per seat because ends and corners go unused. Our free space planning can map the room and give you an exact count for each layout you want to support.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between flip-top and nesting tables?

A flip-top table has a top that tilts to vertical so it takes less floor depth when stored. A nesting table is a flip-top table whose legs and base are designed so several tables slide into each other in a tight row. All nesting tables flip, but not every flip-top table nests efficiently.

What size training table should I buy?

For two people seated side by side, a 60 inch wide table is the common minimum and 72 inches gives more elbow room. Depth of 24 inches suits notebooks and tablets, while 30 inches is better for laptops and printed materials. Choose one standard size so tables can be recombined into any layout.

Do you offer volume pricing for a full training room?

Published volume pricing applies to workstations at 5% for 20 or more, 8% for 50 or more and 12% for 100 or more stations. For training tables, send your full room count and we will quote the room as a single project.

Can I mix new and pre-owned training tables?

You can, but matching finish, height and base style matters more with tables than with most furniture because they are placed edge to edge. If you mix grades, keep one grade per room so rows line up and look consistent.

Is installation required?

Most training tables arrive needing base assembly, and power modules or linking hardware add time. Installation is $95 per workstation with a $495 minimum for workstation projects, and table assembly is quoted with your room order.
