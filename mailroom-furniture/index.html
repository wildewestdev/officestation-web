Mailroom Furniture That Fits How Your Mail Actually Moves

Package volume has changed what an office mailroom needs. Here is how to size sorting stations, work surfaces and storage, and where pre-owned furniture can cut the cost.

6 minute read

Mailroom furniture is easy to overlook until packages pile up on the floor. If your office receives more parcels than it did when the mailroom was designed, a room built around envelope slots struggles with boxes. The right setup is less about buying a mailroom kit and more about matching surfaces, sorting and storage to how mail actually moves through your building.

Start with the workflow, not the catalog

Every mailroom does the same four jobs, in the same order. Plan a zone for each, and the furniture list writes itself.

  1. Receive: carriers drop off. You need a counter or clear landing area near the door.
  2. Process: items are opened, scanned, logged or labeled. You need work surface at a comfortable height.
  3. Sort and hold: mail goes to slots, parcels go to shelving until pickup.
  4. Ship out: outgoing packages are packed and staged. You need supplies storage and a staging table.

Arrange the zones so items move in one direction, from the door toward pickup. A layout where staff carry boxes back past the receiving counter wastes time and creates clutter.

Sizing your mailroom

Guessing at size is how mailrooms end up too small in their first year. Gather a few numbers before you plan.

  • Daily parcels: count for two typical weeks.
  • Peak-day parcels: the end of a quarter, holiday season or the week after a big order cycle.
  • Hold time: how long a package sits before its recipient picks it up.
  • Recipients: the number of individual or department slots you need.
  • Outgoing volume: how many packages leave per day and whether they are packed in the room.
  • Staff: how many people work in the room at the busiest hour.

Size parcel shelving for peak volume multiplied by hold time, not for an average day. Size work surface by the number of people processing at once, with enough length for an open box, a scanner and a label printer side by side.

Heavy boxes change the plan. Shelving that holds binders may not hold cases of product or equipment. Confirm the weight rating of any shelving before you load it, keep the heaviest items on lower shelves and leave a clear path wide enough for a hand truck.

Security and privacy in the mailroom

Mailrooms handle checks, legal documents, personnel files and customer information. Build basic controls into the furniture plan.

  • Lockable cabinets for sensitive incoming items awaiting pickup
  • A door that locks, with the sorting unit inside the room rather than in an open hallway
  • A dedicated, locked bin for documents headed to shredding
  • A pickup counter that keeps visitors from reaching into the sorting area

Ergonomics for repetitive work

Mail processing means lifting, bending and reaching all day. Keep frequently used slots between waist and shoulder height. Store heavy parcels low. Give seated staff adjustable task chairs, and consider anti-fatigue mats at standing counters.

Frequently asked questions

What furniture does a basic office mailroom need?

Most mailrooms need a sorting unit with labeled slots, a counter-height work surface for opening and processing, parcel shelving and a supply storage cabinet. Larger operations add a receiving counter, a staging table for outgoing shipments and secure lockable storage.

What height should a mailroom work surface be?

Most mail processing happens standing, so counter-height surfaces are common. If staff sit for long stretches, a standard desk-height surface with task seating works better. Height-adjustable surfaces let one station serve both.

Can I use pre-owned furniture in a mailroom?

Yes. Work surfaces, storage cabinets, shelving and task seating are all good candidates for pre-owned purchase. Check that surfaces are flat and stable, drawers and locks work, and shelving is rated for the weight you plan to store.

How much space should I plan for a mailroom?

It depends on daily volume, number of recipients and how long packages wait for pickup. Start by counting daily parcels and peak-day parcels, then size shelving for peak volume. Free space planning can turn those numbers into a layout.

Can a workstation be converted into a mailroom station?

Often, yes. A panel-based workstation provides work surface, overhead storage and a defined footprint. Liquidation-grade 6'x6' workstations start at $615 for furniture only, which can be an affordable base for a processing station.
