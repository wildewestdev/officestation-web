Used Herman Miller Resolve: Planning 120-Degree Workstations

Resolve builds workstations from poles, screens and worksurfaces set at 120-degree angles instead of square panels. That geometry changes how you plan, buy and install a used system.

7 minute read

Most cubicle shopping assumes a square grid: 6'x6' stations, right-angle panels, straight aisles. Herman Miller Resolve throws that out. It uses vertical poles as the structure and attaches screens and worksurfaces between them at 120-degree angles, forming clusters of three, six or more people.

That is why buying used Herman Miller Resolve is less about finding a good price per station and more about understanding how the system plans. Get the geometry and parts count right, and a pre-owned Resolve install can deliver an open, collaborative floor at a fraction of the cost of new.

How Resolve plans differently than panels

In a panel system, you draw a rectangle, divide it into squares and run aisles between rows. Resolve works on angles instead, which changes almost every planning decision.

Planning factorTraditional panel systemResolve, 120-degree
StructureWalls carry the load and the worksurfacesPoles carry screens and worksurfaces
Basic unitSquare or rectangular stationAngled station within a cluster
Floor patternRows and a rectilinear gridHoneycomb-style clusters with organic aisles
PrivacySet by panel heightSet by screen height and orientation, generally more open
Fitting odd roomsCan leave awkward leftover spaceClusters can flex around columns and angled walls
ReconfiguringSwap panels within the same gridRethink the cluster, poles and screens together

Two practical consequences follow. First, you cannot convert a panel floor plan to Resolve by replacing each square with a Resolve station. The circulation paths and cluster spacing need to be drawn from scratch. Second, the angled worksurfaces tend to give each person a wide, wraparound work area, so the per-person feel does not map neatly to a 6'x6' comparison.

Plan before you buy. With Resolve, the floor plan decides the parts list. Send us dimensions, headcount and column locations first. Our space planning is free, and it tells you how many poles, screens and worksurfaces your layout actually needs before you commit to a lot.

Count parts, not stations

A used Resolve listing that says "24 stations" tells you very little. What matters is whether every component needed to stand those stations up is there. Ask for, or build, a component inventory that covers:

  1. Poles, including their heights and bases or floor connections.
  2. Beams and connectors that link poles and carry loads between them.
  3. Screens, by size and type, and whether they are fabric or another material.
  4. Worksurfaces and the supports that attach them.
  5. Canopies, shelves and storage if your plan uses them.
  6. Power and cable routing components. Remember that electrical work is not part of furniture pricing.

Missing a handful of connectors can stall an entire cluster. On any lot we sell, we tell you what is included so you are not discovering gaps on install day.

Add spares for future changes

Because Resolve parts are specific to the system, it pays to hold a small reserve of the components that wear or go missing most often, such as connectors and screens. Buying a few extras alongside your main lot is usually easier than hunting for single pieces later, when matching inventory may be gone.

Installation takes system familiarity

Pole-based systems go together differently than panels, and cluster alignment matters for a clean result. Professional installation is $95 per workstation with a $495 minimum, and it is worth considering if your in-house team has only worked with panel cubicles.

Condition grades for pre-owned Resolve

Office Station is an independent reseller of pre-owned Herman Miller furniture, not an authorized dealer, and manufacturer warranties generally do not transfer. Each lot gets our condition grade:

  • Grade A, like new: little or no visible wear.
  • Grade B, very good: light marks such as minor scratches on poles or small surface scuffs.
  • Grade C, good: fully functional with more visible cosmetic wear, such as faded screen fabric.

On pole systems, pay special attention to screen fabric and worksurface edges. Poles usually wear well, while fabric is where age shows first.

When Resolve makes sense, and when it does not

Resolve fits teams that value openness, collaboration and a less corporate look, and floors with irregular shapes where a grid wastes space. It is a weaker fit if people need high privacy, or if you expect to reconfigure often using in-house staff who know panel systems.

Frequently asked questions

How is Resolve different from a panel cubicle?

A panel system uses walls joined at right angles, and the walls usually carry the worksurfaces. Resolve uses vertical poles as the structure, with screens and worksurfaces attached between them at 120-degree angles. The result is clusters that read more like a honeycomb than a grid.

Can I mix Resolve with my existing panel system?

Not directly, since the two structures are not designed to connect. Most offices place them in separate zones and use storage, planters or aisle space as the transition. We can show that on a free space plan.

What is the biggest risk when buying used Resolve?

Incomplete parts. A Resolve cluster depends on poles, connectors, screens and support hardware all being present. Before you buy, get a component count for each station, not just a station count.

Is Office Station a Herman Miller dealer?

No. We are an independent reseller of pre-owned furniture and are not authorized by Herman Miller. Manufacturer warranties generally do not transfer to pre-owned pieces, so we grade each lot A like new, B very good or C good.

How much does a used Resolve workstation cost?

Resolve is quoted per project because component mixes vary. Our standard 6'x6' workstation benchmarks are $615 liquidation-grade, $1,245 remanufactured and $1,920 new, furniture only. Electrical, delivery and installation are extra.
