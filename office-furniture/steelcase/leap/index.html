Used Steelcase Leap Chairs: V1 vs V2 Buying Guide

The Leap is one of the most sought-after task chairs on the pre-owned market, and it comes in two generations. Here is how to tell them apart, what to inspect and how to buy one with realistic expectations.

7 minute read

If you search for a used Steelcase Leap chair, you will quickly run into listings that just say "Leap" and others that say "Leap V2." The difference matters. Both generations are good chairs, but they are not identical, and a seller who does not specify which one you are getting is leaving out useful information.

This guide explains how the two generations compare, how to identify them, what to inspect on any used Leap and what an honest seller should tell you about warranty.

Why the Leap holds its value

The Leap earned its reputation with a back that flexes to follow your spine as you move and a seat that glides forward as you recline, so your eyes stay near your screen instead of tilting away from it. It offers a deep set of adjustments, including lower back firmness, lumbar height, seat depth, recline tension and adjustable arms.

That adjustability is what makes a Leap appealing to people who sit for long hours and want to tune the chair to their body. It is also why a Leap with a failing control is a real problem. More adjustments means more things to check.

Leap V1 vs V2 at a glance

The V2 is the later generation and a refinement of the original design. Rather than rely on memorized differences in a single lever or knob, use the comparison below as a buying framework, then confirm the generation from the chair itself.

How to confirm which version you have

  • Check the label under the seat. It usually includes model and date information. Ask the seller for a clear photo of it.
  • Ask directly. A reputable seller should be able to tell you the generation and point to how they know.
  • Compare controls against photos from the same listing. If a listing mixes photos of different chairs, ask for photos of the exact unit you will receive.
Generation is not the whole story. A well-kept V1 with a smooth mechanism and intact arms can be a better chair than a V2 that spent years in a heavy-use call center. Decide on condition first, then use generation as the tiebreaker.

What condition grades mean on a Leap

Office Station grades each pre-owned chair on a three-level scale.

  • Grade A, like new. Clean upholstery, crisp arm pads and every adjustment working as it should. The right choice when the chair will be used all day by one person.
  • Grade B, very good. Light signs of use, such as minor fabric wear or scuffs on the base, with full function. The practical choice for most offices.
  • Grade C, good. Visible wear, such as worn arm pads or faded fabric, that does not affect function. A budget-friendly option for shared or lighter-use seats.

Because the Leap has so many adjustments, pay attention to the word function in those definitions. A cosmetically clean chair with a lumbar control that will not hold is not a good buy at any grade.

Used Leap inspection checklist

Go through every item, in person or by asking the seller to confirm.

  1. Seat height. Raise and lower fully. The chair should hold its height without slowly sinking over a few minutes.
  2. Seat depth. Slide the seat through its range and confirm it locks.
  3. Recline tension. Adjust from light to firm and feel the difference when you lean back.
  4. Recline limiter. Set a few positions and confirm the back stops where you set it.
  5. Lower back firmness. Adjust and feel for a clear change in support.
  6. Lumbar height. Move it up and down and confirm it stays in place.
  7. Arms. Test every arm adjustment the chair has. Look for cracked or peeling pads and arms that wobble or will not lock.
  8. Seat glide. Recline and notice whether the seat moves forward smoothly.
  9. Upholstery and foam. Check the seat front edge, seams and the top of the back.
  10. Base and casters. Check for smooth rolling and casters that suit your floor.

An honest word about warranty

Office Station is an independent reseller of pre-owned furniture. We are not an authorized Steelcase dealer and are not affiliated with Steelcase. Manufacturer warranties generally do not transfer to pre-owned chairs, whether V1 or V2.

That means your protection comes from a clear grade, real photos of the specific unit and the answers you get before purchase. If a listing implies manufacturer coverage on a used Leap, ask for documentation and verify it with the manufacturer.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the Leap V1 and V2?

The Leap V2 is the later generation of the chair and a refinement of the original design, with updated controls, arms and styling. Both share the core Leap idea of a flexible back and a seat that glides forward as you recline. The simplest way to confirm which one you have is the label under the seat and its model information.

Should I buy a Leap V1 or a V2?

If both are in comparable condition, most buyers prefer the V2 because it is the newer design and replacement parts tend to be easier to find. A clean, well-functioning V1 can still be a good value, especially at a lower price. Condition matters more than generation.

Does a used Leap come with the Steelcase warranty?

Generally, no. Office Station is an independent reseller, not an authorized Steelcase dealer, and manufacturer warranties generally do not transfer to pre-owned furniture. Be cautious of any seller who implies otherwise without documentation.

What wears out first on a used Leap?

The parts that see the most contact usually show wear first: arm pads, seat fabric at the front edge, casters and the gas cylinder. Test the height hold, the recline tension and every arm adjustment before you buy.

Can I order used Leap chairs for a whole team?

You can ask. Pre-owned quantities depend on what has come out of recent office projects, so matching generation and color across a large count may take flexibility. Send your quantity and preferences for an honest answer.
