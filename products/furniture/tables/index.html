Used Conference and Office Tables

Conference, training and meeting tables shape how a room works. Here is how to size them, what to inspect on a pre-owned table, and how delivery and installation are quoted.

6 minute read

Tables are the shared furniture in an office. One conference table might host a board meeting in the morning and a pizza lunch in the afternoon. A training room might need to shift from classroom rows to small groups in fifteen minutes. Buying used conference and office tables lets you furnish those rooms with designer-brand pieces for less, but only if the size, shape and access all line up.

Sizing a conference table

The most common mistake with used conference tables is buying the biggest one that fits through the door. A table that fills the room leaves no space to sit comfortably.

  • Seating: plan roughly 30 inches of edge per person. A 10-foot table seats about eight to ten people depending on the ends.
  • Clearance: leave 36 inches minimum between the table edge and walls, and closer to 48 inches where people walk behind seated guests.
  • Screens: keep enough distance between the far end of the table and a wall-mounted display so the nearest seats are not craning their necks.
  • Shape: rectangular tables fit narrow rooms, racetrack and boat shapes improve sightlines to the ends, and round tables work best for four to six people.

A quick method is to tape the table outline on the floor, set chairs in place and walk around it. If you would rather skip that, our free space planning can lay out the room from a floor plan or dimensions.

What to inspect on a used table

Tables are simpler than seating or sit-stand desks, but a few details make or break a purchase.

  1. All sections present. Multi-piece conference tables need every top section, connector plate and base. A missing center section is hard to replace.
  2. Finish match. Sections should match in color and wear. Wood veneer can shift in tone with sun exposure, so pieces from different rooms may not line up.
  3. Edges and corners. Look for chipped edge banding and lifted veneer, which are common where chairs bump the table.
  4. Power and data. Many conference tables have built-in grommets or power modules. Confirm the module is present and note that electrical connection to the building is not included.
  5. Mechanisms. On training tables, test every flip-top latch, nesting hinge and caster lock.
  6. Level and stable. Leveling glides should adjust, and the table should not rock.

Condition grades for tables

Every pre-owned piece is graded A like new, B very good or C good. For a boardroom where clients sit, most buyers choose Grade A so the surface reads as new. Training rooms and breakrooms are good places for Grade B or C, since those tables take daily wear anyway and the savings add up across a room of twenty.

Plan the path into the room

Large tables are the item most likely to get stuck on delivery day. Before you order, measure:

  • Elevator interior depth, width and door height
  • Door widths along the path, including suite entry and conference room doors
  • Tight hallway turns where a long top section has to pivot
  • Whether the building requires dock scheduling or after-hours deliveries

Share those details with your request. It is far cheaper to plan for a sectional top than to carry one back down.

How do I size a conference table for my room?

A common planning rule is about 30 inches of table edge per seated person, plus 36 to 48 inches from the table edge to the wall so chairs can pull out and people can walk behind them. Send your room dimensions and we will help with free space planning.

Will a large conference table fit in my elevator?
