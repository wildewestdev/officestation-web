Choosing a Reception Desk That Fits Your Lobby

The right reception desk is the one that fits your lobby, your front-desk workflow and your visitors. Measure first, then decide between pre-owned and new.

6 minute read

Your reception desk is the first piece of furniture every visitor sees, and it is also someone's full-time workstation. Getting it wrong shows up fast: a desk that crowds the entry, a counter that is too high to hand anything across, or a receptionist with no room to push back a chair. Choosing a reception desk is mostly a measuring exercise, and this guide walks through it.

Step 1: Measure the lobby before you shop

Sketch your lobby on paper and record these dimensions:

  • Overall width and depth of the reception area.
  • Door locations and swing, including the entry door and any door behind the desk.
  • Walk path from the entry to the desk and from the desk to the office interior.
  • Power and data locations, since the receptionist needs a computer, phone and often a badge printer.
  • Waiting seating you plan to keep, with its footprint.

Mark the desk footprint with painter's tape on the floor. Walk through the space as a visitor and as the receptionist. Tape catches problems that a drawing hides.

Step 2: Plan the clearances

The desk itself is only part of the footprint. Use these planning figures as a starting point, then confirm with your architect, landlord or local code official.

Accessibility is not optional. Public-facing counters generally need an accessible section. The figures above reflect the 2010 ADA Standards for sales and service counters, but requirements can vary by occupancy and jurisdiction. Verify with a qualified professional before you buy.

Step 3: Pick a shape that suits the room

Once you know how much floor you really have, the shape usually picks itself.

Straight desk

Best for narrow entries and small offices with light visitor traffic. It uses the least depth but offers the least storage and work surface.

L-shaped desk

The most common choice for small and mid-size lobbies. The return gives the receptionist a second surface for a printer or paperwork, and the desk can tuck into a corner to keep the walk path open.

U-shaped desk

Suits busy front desks that handle deliveries, check-ins and phones at once. It needs the most depth, so confirm the clearance behind the desk before you commit.

Two-person desk

If two people staff reception at the same time, plan at least one full work surface and chair clearance per person. Shared keyboards and elbow-to-elbow seating wear thin quickly.

Step 4: Match the desk to what reception actually does

List every task that happens at the front desk, then check the desk supports it:

  1. Greeting and signing in visitors: transaction ledge, space for a tablet or sign-in book.
  2. Receiving packages: surface or floor space for boxes that does not block the entry.
  3. Answering phones and email: monitor placement that does not face visitors.
  4. Handling confidential paperwork: a ledge or modesty panel that hides the work surface.
  5. Storage: lockable pedestal drawers for keys, badges and personal items.
  6. Cable management: grommets and a routed path to the nearest outlet.

Step 5: Consider pre-owned before new

Reception desks often see lighter physical use than open-office workstations, which can make them good pre-owned candidates. Availability of any specific desk varies, so share your dimensions and preferred shape and ask what fits.

When you evaluate a pre-owned reception desk, look closely at:

  • The transaction ledge. This is the most-handled surface. Check for chips, scratches and loose fasteners.
  • The front panel. Visitors see it all day. Dents and scuffs here matter more than on the back side.
  • Edge banding. Peeling edges are common on laminate and hard to hide.
  • Drawers and locks. Confirm keys are available or plan to rekey.
  • Handedness. An L-shaped desk with the return on the wrong side may not fit your corner.

Frequently asked questions

How much space do I need behind a reception desk?

Plan roughly 42 to 48 inches between the back of the desk work surface and the wall or nearest obstruction so the receptionist can roll a chair back and stand up. More is better if two people share the desk. Confirm against your own layout and local code.

What height should a reception desk transaction counter be?

Many reception desks use a raised transaction ledge around 42 inches high for visitors who stand. For accessibility, a lowered counter section is also commonly required; the 2010 ADA Standards call for a portion no higher than 36 inches. Check current requirements with your architect or code official.

Is a pre-owned reception desk a good idea?

It can be, since reception desks often see light use compared with workstations. Inspect the transaction ledge, front panel and edge banding closely, because those are the surfaces visitors see. Office Station sells pre-owned furniture as an independent reseller, and manufacturer warranties generally do not transfer.

Should I choose an L-shaped or U-shaped reception desk?

An L-shape suits most small and mid-size lobbies with one receptionist. A U-shape adds storage and work surface for busier desks but needs more depth. A straight desk works in narrow entries where floor space is tight.
