How Much Is Used Office Furniture Worth? Resale Value Explained

Used office furniture is worth what a buyer can resell it for, minus what it costs to get it out of your building. Here is how brand, age, condition, volume and logistics set that number.

11 minute read  ·  Updated September 2026

You paid a lot for your office furniture. So it is natural to expect it to be worth a lot when the lease ends, the company consolidates or the team goes hybrid. The honest answer is usually a surprise: most used office furniture is worth a small fraction of its original price, and some of it is worth less than the cost of carrying it out the door.

This guide explains exactly why, and how to estimate your own number before you call anyone. The figures come from Office Station's current model, the same logic behind our liquidation estimator. They are our own working numbers, not industry statistics, and every real project is priced from an actual inventory and site visit.

The short answer

Used office furniture value comes down to one equation:

What you receive = recovery value - removal cost

  • Recovery value is what a buyer can pay per item, set by brand tier, age and condition.
  • Removal cost is the labor and trucking to empty the space, set by volume, building access and your deadline.

If recovery is higher, you get paid. If they are close, the removal is effectively free. If removal is higher, you pay the difference. The original invoice, the depreciation schedule and what a new chair costs today do not enter the equation at all.

Resale value is not retail value. The price you see on a used furniture showroom floor is what a refurbished, delivered, warrantied piece sells for. What that same piece is worth sitting in your office, unremoved and uninspected, is a different and much smaller number.

Why liquidators pay far less than retail

It is easy to look at a used chair listing and assume a liquidator is pocketing the difference. In practice, the gap between buy price and sell price is where most of the work and risk of the business lives. Between your floor and a buyer's floor, a liquidator carries:

  • Storage. Workstations and conference tables take up a lot of warehouse space, and they may sit for months before the right buyer needs that exact configuration.
  • Refurbishing. Cleaning, replacing worn parts, touching up finishes, swapping fabric and sorting mismatched components all cost labor and materials.
  • Sales time. Photographing, listing, quoting, space planning and answering questions takes staff time for every piece.
  • Risk. Some inventory never sells. Finishes go out of style, demand shifts, and unsold furniture eventually has to be recycled or disposed of at the liquidator's cost.

For context, Office Station's liquidation-grade 6'x6' workstation sells for $615, before delivery and installation. A good-condition premium workstation under eight years old recovers $110 in our model. The distance between those two numbers is the storage, refurbishing, parts, sales time and unsold-inventory risk described above.

Brand tier: the biggest value driver

Brand matters more than any other single factor because it decides whether a resale market exists. Buyers search for specific manufacturers, parts are available for them, and matching pieces can be added to an existing installation. Office Station groups furniture into three tiers:

  • Premium: Herman Miller, Steelcase, Knoll and Haworth.
  • Commercial: Allsteel, Kimball, Teknion, HON, AIS and Friant.
  • Unbranded: everything without a recognizable resale market.

Age and condition

Age

In the table above, value falls by roughly half or more at each age step. Newer furniture matches current finishes and layouts, parts are easier to source, and buyers can add to it for years. Once furniture passes 15 years, only premium pieces keep meaningful value, and even those are worth a fraction of what they were.

If a move or consolidation is likely in the next year or two, age is worth watching. Furniture that crosses the eight-year line before you sell it drops into the next column.

Condition

Condition multiplies the per-item value:

  • Good (clean, complete, fully functional): x1.0
  • Fair (normal wear, minor stains or scuffs): x0.75
  • Heavy wear (damage, missing parts, heavy staining): x0.4

Volume cuts both ways. A larger project spreads the $750 mobilization across more items, and a floor of matching furniture is easier to resell than a mix of one-offs. But volume also multiplies the per-item removal cost, so a big floor of low-value furniture becomes a big bill.

Access and time matter just as much. A loading dock and a four-week runway keep costs at the base rate. Elevator-only access, stairs, or a deadline measured in days all raise the removal side without changing the value of a single chair. Three worked examples

Each example is the same 40-person office: 40 workstations, 45 task chairs, 4 private offices and 2 conference rooms. Removal for that inventory at base rates is $750 + $2,600 + $270 + $380 + $160 = $4,160.

Example 1: Newer premium furniture, good condition

Premium brands, under eight years old, good condition, loading dock, more than four weeks.

  • Recovery: $4,400 + $2,700 + $560 + $220 = $7,880
  • Removal: $4,160
  • Net: about $3,720 paid to you

Example 2: The same premium furniture, older and worn

Premium brands, 8 to 15 years old, fair condition, loading dock, more than four weeks.

  • Recovery: $1,650 + $1,012.50 + $195 + $82.50 = $2,940
  • Removal: $4,160
  • Net: you pay about $1,220

Same brands, same headcount. One age step and a shift from good to fair moved the result by almost $5,000.

Example 3: Commercial furniture in a harder building

Commercial brands, 8 to 15 years old, fair condition, elevator-only access, a two-to-four-week deadline.

  • Recovery: $750 + $202.50 + $90 + $30 = $1,072.50
  • Removal: $4,160 x 1.15 x 1.1 = $5,262.40
  • Net: you pay about $4,190
A net cost is not a bad outcome. In Example 3, recovery value still takes about $1,070 off the bill compared with hauling everything away for nothing. For older commercial furniture, the realistic goal is the lowest net cost, delivered on schedule.

Why selling piecemeal rarely works for full floors

When the numbers above feel low, listing furniture yourself on a marketplace is tempting. For a handful of premium chairs or a single executive desk, it can work. For a full floor, it rarely does, for reasons that have nothing to do with effort:

  • Systems furniture needs skilled disassembly. Panels, powered bases, connectors and worksurfaces come apart in a specific order. Most private buyers cannot do it, and parts get lost when they try.
  • Buyers cherry-pick. The best chairs sell first. You are left with the panels, pedestals and odd pieces that nobody wanted, which still need removal.
  • Removal cost barely shrinks. A crew still mobilizes for the leftovers, and you lose the matched-set value a liquidator would have paid for the whole floor.
  • Buildings restrict access. Many properties require certificates of insurance, scheduled freight elevator time and escorts. That is hard to arrange for dozens of individual buyers.
  • No-shows cost time. Every missed pickup burns hours of staff time, and your lease end date does not move.

If you do try, sell only the high-value pieces, set a firm cutoff date well ahead of your removal crew, and tell your liquidator exactly what will already be gone.

How to get the most for your furniture

  1. Start early. Four weeks or more keeps you out of the deadline multipliers, and more than that gives time to plan resale.
  2. Build an accurate inventory. Count workstations, chairs, offices and conference rooms, note brands and approximate age, and take clear photos. A quote built on real information does not change on removal day.
  3. Keep sets together. Matching workstations and chairs are worth more as a group than as scattered pieces.
  4. Protect condition. Gather keys, keep connectors and hardware with the furniture, and stop anyone from dismantling pieces early.
  5. Secure access. Ask the property manager about dock hours and freight elevator windows before accepting an elevator-only plan.
  6. Compare net numbers. Ask every bidder for the net outcome for the whole space, including what is excluded.

Frequently asked questions

How much is used office furniture worth?

Far less than it cost new, and sometimes nothing. In Office Station's current model, a good-condition premium workstation under eight years old recovers about $110, while an unbranded workstation over 15 years old recovers $0. Brand tier, age and condition set the per-item value, and removal cost decides whether you get paid, break even or pay.

What brands of office furniture have the best resale value?

Premium manufacturers such as Herman Miller, Steelcase, Knoll and Haworth hold value best because buyers recognize them and parts are available. Commercial brands such as Allsteel, Kimball, Teknion, HON, AIS and Friant resell for less. Unbranded furniture usually has little or no resale market.

Why do liquidators pay so little for office furniture?

A liquidator pays for furniture before it has been removed, stored, refurbished or sold. The difference between the purchase price and the eventual sale price has to cover trucking, warehouse space, cleaning and parts, sales time and the risk that some pieces never sell. The original purchase price does not factor in.

Should I sell used office furniture on Craigslist or Facebook Marketplace?

For a few premium chairs or a single desk, a marketplace sale can work. For a full floor it rarely does, because workstations need skilled disassembly, buyers often do not show up, your building may restrict access, and everything that does not sell still has to be removed before your deadline.

Does condition really change resale value that much?

Yes. In Office Station's model, fair condition is worth 75 percent of good condition and heavy wear is worth 40 percent. On a floor of premium furniture, that can be the difference between receiving a check and paying for removal.

How old is too old for office furniture to have resale value?

Value drops at each age step in Office Station's model: under 8 years, 8 to 15 years, and over 15 years. Premium furniture still carries some value past 15 years, but commercial and unbranded furniture that old is usually worth little or nothing to a buyer.

Can I get paid to have my office furniture removed?

You can when the recovery value of your furniture is higher than the removal cost. That most often happens with newer premium furniture in good condition, in a building with dock access and a reasonable deadline. Run your inventory through the estimator to see where your space lands.
