Cubicle Size Guide: Standard Dimensions and Space Planning

Pick the right cubicle size, then find out how many actually fit in your space once aisles, accessibility and support rooms are counted.

10 minute read  ·  Updated September 2026

Cubicle size decisions ripple through everything else: how many people fit, how the room feels, what you spend and whether your layout passes inspection. This guide gives you the standard dimensions, the math planners actually use, and three sample layouts you can adapt.

Standard cubicle sizes

Cubicle sizes are quoted as width by depth in feet, measured to the panel centerlines. The usable interior is a few inches smaller once panel thickness is counted, so a 6'x6' station gives you slightly less than 36 clear square feet.

SizeFootprintZone sq ft per station*Best for
Open bench seatabout 5' wide x 2.5' deep25-35Collaborative teams, hoteling, hybrid staff
5'x5' compact25 sq ft38-48Call centers, data entry, single-monitor roles
6'x6' standard36 sq ft50-60Most full-time desk roles
6'x8' large48 sq ft60-75Dual monitors, paper-heavy work, guest chair
8'x8' manager64 sq ft85-100Supervisors, small meetings at the desk

*Zone square feet assumes stations placed back to back in pods, with a 48-inch aisle shared between pods. It covers the workstation area only, not conference rooms, break rooms or main corridors. The math is explained below.

A standard 6'x6' usually includes an L-shaped worksurface, overhead or tackboard options, and room for a mobile pedestal under the desk. It is the workhorse of most offices and the size most commonly available in liquidation inventory. Pricing by size is covered in the used cubicle price guide.

Panel heights

Panel height changes how a floor feels more than footprint does. Heights vary by manufacturer, but they fall into three practical bands.

HeightApprox. heightSeatedStandingUse it for
Low47"Can see neighbors over the panelFull view of the floorCollaborative teams, open feel, natural light
Mid53"Visual privacyCan see across the floorGeneral office work, the most common choice
Tall65"Full privacyLimited view over panelsPhone-heavy roles, focus work, finance and HR

A common approach is to mix heights: tall panels on the spine between back-to-back stations, where they block the view and sound of the person behind you, and low or mid panels facing the aisle. Keep tall panels away from exterior windows so daylight still reaches the interior of the floor.

Check sprinkler and light clearance. Very tall panels and stacked toppers can interfere with sprinkler coverage, thermostat readings and light distribution in some spaces. If you are going above typical tall-panel heights, run the layout past your landlord or fire marshal.

Choosing the right size by role

Start with how people work, not with how many you want to fit.

  • Customer support and sales floors: 5'x5' or 6'x6' with tall or mid panels. Acoustic separation matters more than surface area.
  • General administrative and professional staff: 6'x6' with mid panels. Enough surface for dual monitors and some paper.
  • Accounting, design, engineering and legal support: 6'x8'. Large monitors, plans and files need the extra depth.
  • Supervisors and team leads: 8'x8'. Room for a guest chair and quick one-on-one conversations without booking a room.
  • Hybrid and rotating staff: bench seating. People who are in two or three days a week rarely need a dedicated cubicle.

Mixing sizes in one layout is normal. Keeping all stations in a pod the same size makes installation and future reconfiguration simpler.

Space-planning math

Here is how to estimate capacity yourself before you pay for a formal plan.

Step 1: Workstation zone per station

Picture a pod of 6'x6' stations placed back to back. Two rows are 12 feet deep. Add a 4-foot (48-inch) aisle that serves that pod and the next one, and each repeating module is 16 feet deep. Every 6 feet of width holds two stations, one on each side of the spine.

  • Module area: 6 ft x 16 ft = 96 sq ft
  • Stations in that module: 2
  • Zone area per station: 96 / 2 = 48 sq ft

Real layouts lose some space at pod ends, columns and walls, so a planning range of 50-60 square feet per 6'x6' is realistic. The same method gives about 60-75 for a 6'x8', 85-100 for an 8'x8', and 25-35 per bench seat.

Step 2: Add main circulation

Main corridors connect pods to exits, restrooms, and support rooms. Plan on roughly 15-20% of your usable space for primary circulation, more on floors with an awkward shape or many columns.

Step 3: Add support space

Budget space for what every office needs regardless of headcount:

  • Reception or entry: 120-200 sq ft
  • Small conference room for 6-8: 150-250 sq ft
  • Private office: 100-150 sq ft each
  • Break room or kitchen: 150-300 sq ft
  • Copy, storage and IT: 80-150 sq ft

Step 4: Check your total per person

Divide usable square feet by headcount. As rules of thumb, not code, an office built on bench seating runs about 80-110 square feet per person, a cubicle-based office about 110-175, and an office with many private offices 175-250 or more. If your number falls below the range for your layout type, the plan is probably too tight.

Aisle widths and accessibility

Aisles are where layouts fail inspections and where offices feel cramped. Build these numbers into your plan from the start.

ClearancePlanning figureNotes
Accessible route36" minimum clearADA Standards minimum for an accessible route
Wheelchair turning space60" diameter or a T-shaped turnNeeded where a route changes direction or dead-ends
Doorway clear width32" minimum clearMeasured with the door open 90 degrees
Aisle between workstation pods42-48" typicalComfortable for two people passing
Egress corridor44" is commonVaries by occupancy and jurisdiction; check local code

Clear width means clear. Measure from the face of a panel, not the centerline, and remember that chairs, pedestals and trash bins push into aisles. A 36-inch aisle on paper often behaves like a 30-inch aisle once people are working.

For employee work areas, the general expectation is that people with disabilities can approach, enter and exit the area, and employers make reasonable accommodations for individual employees. Height-adjustable worksurfaces and a few larger stations near an accessible route make those accommodations easy later.

Codes are local. The figures above are planning guidance, not legal advice. Egress widths, occupant load and exit counts depend on your building, occupancy classification and jurisdiction. Confirm your final layout with your architect, landlord or local building department.

Sample layouts: 2,500, 5,000 and 10,000 sq ft

These examples use usable square feet and the planning ranges above. Your columns, windows and core location will shift the numbers, but they give you a solid starting point.

2,500 sq ft16-18 x 6'x6'1 private office, 1 conference room, reception, break areaabout 130-150
5,000 sq ft28 x 6'x6', 6 x 6'x8', 2 x 8'x8'2 private offices, 1 conference room, 1 huddle room, break room, storageabout 130
10,000 sq ft54 x 6'x6', 10 x 6'x8', 8 x 8'x8'4 private offices, 2 conference rooms, 2 huddle rooms, kitchen, IT and storageabout 130

2,500 square feet

Reserve about 400 square feet for primary circulation, 200 for reception, 200 for a conference room, 130 for a private office, 150 for a break area and 100 for copy and storage. That leaves roughly 1,300 square feet of workstation zone. At 50-60 square feet per 6'x6', you fit about 21-26 stations in theory, but pod ends and walls usually bring it down to 16-18 in a clean layout. Total headcount: 17-19 including the private office.

5,000 square feet

With about 850 square feet of circulation and 1,450 for support rooms, you have roughly 2,700 square feet of zone. Twenty-eight 6'x6' stations use about 1,540, six 6'x8' stations about 405, and two 8'x8' stations about 185, totaling around 2,130 and leaving margin for pod ends and columns. That is 36 stations plus 2 private offices, or 38 people.

10,000 square feet

Plan about 1,700 square feet of circulation and 2,900 for support. The remaining 5,400 square feet of zone holds 54 standard stations (about 2,970), 10 large stations (about 675) and 8 manager stations (about 740), with room to spare for irregular geometry. That is 72 stations plus 4 private offices, or 76 people. Swapping some 6'x6' pods for bench seating could add 15-20 seats for hybrid staff.

Planning for hybrid attendance

If your team is not in the office every day, planning one dedicated cubicle per employee overbuilds the space. Size to realistic peak attendance instead.

  1. Count who is truly full time on site. Reception, operations, finance and on-site managers usually need assigned stations.
  2. Estimate peak hybrid attendance. Look at your busiest day of the week, not the average. If 30 hybrid staff peak at 20 in the office, plan for 20 seats plus a small buffer.
  3. Put hybrid staff on bench seating or shared 6'x6' stations. Bench seats use roughly half the zone space of a standard cubicle, so the savings add up quickly.
  4. Reinvest the saved space. Hybrid teams come in to meet, so swap some workstation area for huddle rooms, phone booths and a larger conference room.

A worked example: an office with 20 full-time staff and 30 hybrid staff peaking at 20 in the office could plan 20 standard 6'x6' stations (about 1,100 square feet of zone) plus 24 bench seats (about 720 square feet), instead of 50 cubicles (about 2,750 square feet). That frees roughly 900 square feet for meeting space or a smaller lease.

Keep in mind that storage moves with people. Hybrid staff at bench seats need lockers or shared storage somewhere, so reserve space for it in the support budget.

Common planning mistakes

  • Planning to gross square footage. Your lease lists rentable square feet, which includes shared building areas. Plan to usable square feet.
  • Ignoring columns. One badly placed column can remove a pair of stations. Measure them and mark them on the plan.
  • Forgetting power and data locations. Powered panels need a feed point from a wall, floor or ceiling. Where the power comes in affects where pods can go.
  • Squeezing aisles to add seats. Two extra stations are not worth a failed inspection or a floor people hate walking through.
  • Planning only for today. Leave 10-15% room to grow, or choose a system you can reconfigure easily.

If you are relocating, plan the new layout before you move. Frequently asked questions

What is the standard cubicle size?

The most common cubicle size is 6'x6', which gives one person 36 square feet with room for an L-shaped worksurface and a pedestal. 6'x8' stations are common for roles that handle paper or dual monitors, and 8'x8' stations are typical for managers. Compact 5'x5' stations and open bench seats are used in dense layouts.

How many cubicles fit in 1,000 square feet?

In a pure workstation zone with 48-inch aisles, a 6'x6' station needs roughly 50-60 square feet including its share of aisle, so about 16-20 stations fit in 1,000 square feet. Once you add a conference room, break area and main circulation, plan on 110-175 square feet of total usable space per person.

How much space should I plan per employee?

A useful rule of thumb is 80-110 usable square feet per person for bench seating, 110-175 for a cubicle-based office, and 175-250 or more for an office with many private offices. These are planning ranges, not code requirements, so confirm your final layout with a space planner or architect.

How wide do aisles between cubicles need to be?

Under the ADA Standards, an accessible route must be at least 36 inches clear. Many offices plan 42-48 inch aisles between workstation rows for comfort, and exit corridors are commonly required to be 44 inches or wider. Egress requirements vary, so check local code and your building's requirements.

What are standard cubicle panel heights?

Office Station groups panels into three heights: low at about 47 inches, mid at about 53 inches and tall at about 65 inches. Exact heights vary by manufacturer. Low panels keep sightlines open, mid panels give seated privacy, and tall panels provide the most visual and acoustic separation.

Is 5'x5' too small for a cubicle?

A 5'x5' station works for task-focused roles with a single monitor and minimal paper, such as call center or data entry work. It gets tight with dual monitors, an L-shaped surface and a pedestal. If people spend a full day at their desks, a 6'x6' is usually the better minimum.
