Used vs Remanufactured vs New Cubicles: An Honest Comparison

Three grades of cubicle, three very different price tags. Here is what you actually get with each, where the money goes, and how to pick the right one for your office.

9 minute read  ·  Updated September 2026

Every cubicle project eventually hits the same question: do you buy used, remanufactured or new? The honest answer depends on your budget, your timeline, how your office needs to look, and how long you plan to keep the furniture. This guide lays out the real differences without the sales spin.

The three grades, defined

Used (liquidation-grade)

Liquidation-grade cubicles come out of real offices that are closing, downsizing or relocating. At Office Station, that means the furniture is cleaned and inspected, then sold with the finishes it arrived with. You get the original fabric, laminate and trim, with whatever normal wear a working office leaves behind.

What you should expect: solid structure, working components and honest cosmetic wear. What you should not expect: a showroom look. If a seller calls used furniture "like new" without replacing anything, ask to see it in person.

Remanufactured

Remanufactured cubicles start as used systems furniture, but the parts people see and touch are replaced. The steel frames and core panel structure are reused. The fabric, worksurfaces, edge banding and hardware are new, and frames are refinished where needed. The result looks new, matches throughout, and costs a fraction of new.

"Refurbished" is often used interchangeably with remanufactured, but it can mean anything from a thorough cleaning to a full rebuild. Always ask what specifically was replaced.

New

New cubicles are built from new materials by a commercial manufacturer and ordered to your specification. You choose from the full range of current fabrics, finishes and features, and you get the manufacturer's warranty. You also wait for production and freight, and you pay the most.

What remanufacturing actually replaces

A real remanufacturing process goes component by component. Here is what gets replaced or restored, and what stays.

ComponentLiquidation-gradeRemanufactured
Panel fabricOriginal, cleanedReplaced with new fabric
WorksurfacesOriginal laminate, inspectedReplaced or resurfaced with new laminate
Edge bandingOriginalReplaced
Frames, trim and metal partsOriginal finishRepainted or powder coated as needed
Hardware (hinges, slides, locks, keys, levelers, connectors)Original, checked for functionWorn or missing pieces replaced
Steel panel framesReusedReused
Electrical componentsReused if present, inspectedReused if present, inspected

Why these parts

Fabric and laminate edges take the daily abuse: rolling chairs, coffee, sun and the occasional box cutter. Hinges, drawer slides and locks are the moving parts that loosen over years of use. Steel frames, on the other hand, rarely wear out in normal office use. Remanufacturing spends money where wear actually happens and reuses what is still sound.

What to ask about electrical

Powered panels contain base raceways, harnesses and receptacles. Ask whether electrical components were inspected, whether all parts come from the same manufacturer's system, and plan for a licensed electrician to make the building connection. Mixing electrical components across systems is a bad idea even when parts look similar.

Get it in writing. Ask any seller for a written list of what their remanufacturing includes. If the answer is vague, or it does not mention fabric, worksurfaces, edge banding and hardware, you may be buying cleaned used furniture at a remanufactured price.

Lifespan and durability

Systems furniture from the major commercial manufacturers was engineered for reconfiguration. The panels are designed to be taken down, moved and rebuilt as companies grow and reorganize. That is exactly why used and remanufactured cubicles work: the design anticipates more than one life.

How long each grade lasts in your office depends on a few things:

  • The original quality of the system. A major-manufacturer frame reused is often more durable than a cheaply built new station. Grade and quality are not the same thing.
  • How it was handled. Furniture that was carefully decommissioned, labeled and stored holds up better than furniture pulled apart in a rush.
  • Cosmetic tolerance. Liquidation-grade fabric and laminates have already used some of their cosmetic life. The structure is fine; the look ages sooner.
  • Your use. A 24-hour call center wears out chairs and worksurfaces faster than a professional office that is half-empty on Fridays.

In practice, remanufactured furniture resets the cosmetic clock on the surfaces that wear, and new furniture resets everything. Liquidation-grade gives you the most furniture for the money with the shortest cosmetic runway.

Warranty considerations

This is the area where buyers most often make assumptions. A few realities:

  • Manufacturer warranties on new furniture apply to the original purchaser and typically do not transfer when the furniture is resold.
  • Used and remanufactured warranties come from the seller, and terms vary widely. Some sellers offer none.
  • Labor may be excluded even when parts are covered. Ask who pays to reinstall a replaced part.
  • Chairs often carry separate terms from workstations, especially mechanisms and cylinders.

Ask for warranty terms in writing before you sign, and ask what happens if a component fails in the first month. A seller that stocks matching parts can usually fix a problem quickly, which matters as much as the warranty wording.

Parts availability is a hidden warranty. Buy systems furniture where matching panels, worksurfaces and connectors are still available. Then expansions, repairs and reconfigurations stay simple for years, whichever grade you choose.

Lead times

Timing is often the deciding factor, especially when a lease date is fixed.

  • Liquidation-grade: in as little as 72 hours when in stock at Office Station.
  • Remanufactured: usually 7-14 days.
  • New: typically 4-6 weeks time to your location.

The catch with used and remanufactured is quantity. A large matched set is only available if it is in inventory. If you need 100 identical stations by a specific date, reserve early. If you need them next week, in-stock liquidation-grade is your only realistic option. Local buyers can read more about timing in the Phoenix office furniture buying guide.

Sustainability

The most sustainable cubicle is one that already exists. Reusing furniture avoids the raw materials, manufacturing energy and long-haul freight involved in producing new pieces, and it keeps bulky steel, particleboard and fabric out of the waste stream.

  • Liquidation-grade reuses nearly everything.
  • Remanufactured reuses the heaviest components, the steel frames, and replaces the lighter wear surfaces.
  • New starts from new materials, though many manufacturers publish recycled-content and environmental data you can review.

If your company reports on sustainability goals, ask your seller for documentation of what you purchased and its grade. Just as important: when your old office closes, send the furniture to office furniture liquidation instead of a dumpster. The guide to liquidating office furniture explains how.

How to verify what you are buying

Grade labels are only as good as the seller using them. Before you commit, check the furniture against the grade you are paying for.

If you are buying liquidation-grade

  • Ask where it came from. A lot pulled from one office is more likely to match in fabric, laminate and trim than a mixed pile.
  • Look at the worst pieces, not the best. Sellers stage the cleanest station up front. Ask to see several at random.
  • Check edges and low fabric. Front worksurface edges and the bottom foot of each panel show wear first.
  • Count the parts. Make sure connectors, brackets, end caps and leveling glides are included for every station, not just panels and tops.

If you are buying remanufactured

  • Look for consistency. New fabric and laminate should match across every station in the order.
  • Check the frames under the fabric line. Refinished metal should be even, with no chipped old paint showing at corners.
  • Open every drawer and door on a sample. Replaced hardware should feel tight and move smoothly.
  • Ask to see the finish samples before production, so you know exactly what fabric and laminate you are getting.

If you are buying new

  • Confirm the ship date in writing, not just the lead-time estimate.
  • Clarify who receives and inspects freight when it arrives, and who handles damage claims.
  • Get the warranty document for each product line, since chairs, panels and worksurfaces can differ.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between refurbished and remanufactured cubicles?

The terms are used loosely, so always ask what was done. Refurbished often means cleaned with minor repairs or touch-ups. Remanufactured should mean the furniture was taken down to its components, with wear surfaces like fabric, worksurfaces, edge banding and hardware replaced and frames repainted or powder coated as needed.

How long do used cubicles last?

Systems furniture from major manufacturers is built on steel panel frames designed to be installed, taken down and reconfigured many times, so the structure often outlasts its first office by many years. The parts that wear out are fabric, laminate edges, keys, locks and moving hardware. Inspect those, and a well-made used system can serve a long second life.

Do used or remanufactured cubicles come with a warranty?

Original manufacturer warranties usually do not transfer to a second owner, and coverage on used and remanufactured furniture depends entirely on the seller. Ask for warranty terms in writing, including what is covered, for how long, and whether labor is included.

Are remanufactured cubicles as good as new?

Structurally, a quality remanufactured station built on a major manufacturer's frame can perform like new, and it looks new on the surfaces people see and touch. It will not have the very latest design details or a manufacturer's new-product warranty. For most offices, the trade is worth about two-thirds the price of new.

How fast can I get cubicles?

At Office Station, in-stock liquidation-grade stations can be ready in as little as 72 hours, and remanufactured stations usually take 7-14 days. New commercial furniture typically takes 4-6 weeks, and freight to your location can add more time.

Are used cubicles more sustainable than new?

Generally, yes. Reusing steel frames, panels and worksurfaces avoids the materials, manufacturing and freight needed to make new furniture and keeps usable furniture out of the landfill. Remanufacturing replaces some materials, but still reuses the heaviest components.
