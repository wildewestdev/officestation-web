Used Steelcase Leap Buying Guide: V1 vs V2, Wear Points and Replicas

A pre-owned Leap can be one of the best values in office seating, or an expensive mistake. Here is how to tell the generations apart, what to inspect and how to avoid replicas.

10 minute read  ·  Updated September 2026

The Steelcase Leap has been a fixture in corporate offices for years, which means a steady supply of pre-owned chairs comes out of office liquidations. That is good news if you want a high-end task chair without paying new list price. It also means the used market is full of chairs in very different condition, from two different generations, alongside look-alikes that are not Steelcase at all.

This guide walks through what matters when you buy a used or refurbished Leap: the V1 vs V2 question, the features to test, the parts that wear, how to check labels and what condition grades should tell you. Why a used Leap makes sense

Commercial task chairs are designed for years of full-day use by many different people. The core of a Leap, meaning the frame, the back and the recline mechanism, is built for that kind of duty. The parts that take the most abuse, such as casters, arm caps and the gas cylinder, are the kind of components that can be replaced.

That combination is what makes pre-owned designer seating worthwhile. A well-kept chair from a corporate floor can deliver the same adjustability as a new one, at a price that reflects its age and wear. The risk is not the chair design. The risk is buying a specific chair without knowing its generation, its condition or whether it is authentic.

Buy the chair, not the listing. Two used Leaps with the same title can be very different chairs. Generation, foam condition, fabric and the state of every adjustment matter more than the name on the listing.

Leap V1 vs V2

Steelcase has produced two main generations of the Leap. The later generation is commonly called Leap V2, and the earlier one is usually called V1. Both share the core idea that made the chair popular: a backrest designed to move with your spine and a seat that adjusts to fit your body.

V2 was a refinement rather than a completely different chair. The most practical differences show up in how you adjust it. Here is a general comparison of what buyers tend to notice.

AreaLeap V1Leap V2
GenerationEarlier generationLater, refined generation
Seat depth adjustmentAdjustable, with an earlier mechanismRedesigned seat depth mechanism
Arm adjustmentsAdjustable, with an earlier arm designUpdated arms with refined adjustments
Controls and labelingEarlier control layoutUpdated control layout and labeling
Age on the used marketGenerally older chairsGenerally newer chairs
Typical buyer preferenceBudget option if condition is strongUsually the preferred generation

Because V1 chairs are generally older, age alone makes wear more likely. A V1 is not automatically a bad buy, but it should be priced and graded accordingly, and every adjustment should be tested before purchase.

How to identify a V2

Identification is where many buyers get tripped up, so it pays to be careful. No single visual cue is a guarantee, and a chair can have replaced parts. Use several checks together:

  1. Read the manufacturer label. Turn the chair over and look under the seat. An intact Steelcase label with model and manufacturing information is your best starting point.
  2. Look at the seat depth controls. The seat depth mechanism is one of the clearest generational differences. Compare it against current Steelcase product documentation for the Leap.
  3. Check the arms and control labeling. V2 updated the arms and the way controls are presented. Again, compare with manufacturer documentation rather than a marketplace photo.
  4. Ask the seller directly. A reputable seller should be able to tell you which generation a chair is and show you how they know.

If a seller calls a chair a V2 but cannot produce a clear photo of the label or explain the identification, treat the claim as unconfirmed.

The features to test

LiveBack

LiveBack is Steelcase's name for the Leap's flexible backrest, which is designed to change shape as you move so your back stays supported. On a used chair, sit back and shift your posture. The backrest should flex smoothly and quietly, without clicks, grinding or a spot that feels collapsed.

Recline and tension

Lean back through the full recline. The motion should feel controlled, the tension adjustment should noticeably change resistance, and any recline stops should engage and release cleanly. A chair that snaps back, sticks or ignores the tension setting needs a closer look.

Seat depth

Seat depth is one of the Leap's most valuable adjustments, because it lets shorter and taller people get proper thigh support. Move the seat through its full range. It should slide freely and lock firmly at each position. A seat depth control that slips is a common reason to pass on a chair.

Lower back firmness

The Leap lets you adjust how firmly the lower back pushes forward, along with lumbar positioning. Turn the firmness control through its range while seated. You should feel a clear difference. If nothing changes, the mechanism may need service.

Arm adjustments

Test every arm adjustment on both sides: height, width, pivot and any forward or back movement the chair offers. Each should move smoothly and hold its position under light pressure. Uneven or loose arms are easy to miss in a quick sit test.

Seat height

Raise and lower the seat several times, then sit for a few minutes. The seat should hold its height without slowly sinking, which is a sign of a failing cylinder.

What wears out

Every used chair has some wear. What matters is where it is, how severe it is and whether it can be addressed. These are the areas to inspect on any pre-owned Leap.

ComponentWhat to look forWhy it matters
Arm capsCracks, peeling, tears, hardening or rough edgesArm caps are touched constantly and show age quickly. They are typically replaceable.
Seat foamFlattening, a sagging front edge, or feeling the seat pan when seatedCompressed foam reduces comfort over a full day and is not always visible.
Gas cylinderSlow sinking, a seat that will not stay up, or a stiff leverA failing cylinder makes the chair unusable at the right height. It is a replaceable part.
CastersWobble, flat spots, cracked wheels, or the wrong type for your floorCasters affect how the chair rolls and can damage hard floors if mismatched.
FabricStains, pilling, fading, tears or odorFabric condition drives appearance and hygiene. Reupholstery is possible but adds cost.
MechanismNoises, looseness or adjustments that do not holdMechanism issues are the most important to catch before purchase.
Sit for more than ten seconds. Foam compression and cylinder sink often do not show up in a quick sit. Spend a few minutes in the chair, change positions and adjust everything before deciding.

Replicas and manufacturer labels

Popular designer chairs attract imitations, and the Leap is no exception. Some are sold openly as look-alikes. Others show up in consumer listings with a brand name attached and a price that seems too good to be true. Here is how to protect yourself:

  • Check the label under the seat. Look for an intact manufacturer label with model and manufacturing information. A missing, torn or unreadable label does not prove a chair is fake, but it removes your best evidence that it is genuine.
  • Look at build quality. Genuine commercial chairs have consistent materials, clean molding and tight fit between components. Rough plastic, loose joints and flimsy adjustments are warning signs.
  • Test the adjustments. A replica may resemble the Leap without offering the same range or feel of adjustment.
  • Consider the source. Chairs that come out of corporate offices in matched sets have a clearer history than a single chair from an unknown seller.
  • Be wary of the price. A price far below what the condition would suggest deserves more questions, not faster action.

Condition grades A, B and C

A condition grade is only useful if you know what it means. Office Station grades every pre-owned chair after inspection:

  • Grade A: like new.
  • Grade B: light, normal wear.
  • Grade C: visible cosmetic wear, but fully functional.

For a personal chair where appearance matters, Grade A or B is usually the right call. For a training room, a call center or a large team where budget matters more than cosmetics, Grade C can be a smart buy, as long as every function works. Whatever seller you use, ask how they define their grades and whether mechanisms are tested before grading.

Buying for a team

If you are outfitting more than a few seats, matched sets matter. Chairs from the same corporate floor are more likely to share a generation, fabric and color, which keeps a workspace looking consistent. Pairing seating with pre-owned office furniture or used cubicles can also simplify delivery and installation in Greater Phoenix.

Warranty on a pre-owned Leap

Manufacturer warranties are generally written for the original purchaser. As a result, the Steelcase warranty generally does not transfer when a chair is resold, regardless of how new the chair is. Review Steelcase's current warranty terms if this matters to your decision.

That makes the seller's own policies more important. Before you buy, ask in writing what the seller stands behind, how returns work and how problems found at delivery are handled.

Pre-purchase checklist

  1. Confirm the generation using the label and the controls, not just the listing title.
  2. Photograph or inspect the manufacturer label under the seat.
  3. Test the LiveBack flex, recline and tension.
  4. Move the seat depth through its full range and confirm it locks.
  5. Adjust lower back firmness and feel the difference.
  6. Test every arm adjustment on both sides.
  7. Sit for several minutes to check foam and cylinder sink.
  8. Inspect arm caps, casters and fabric closely.
  9. Ask for the condition grade definition and the seller's written policies.
  10. For multiple chairs, confirm they match in generation, fabric and color.

Frequently asked questions

Is a used Steelcase Leap worth buying?

A used Leap in good condition can be an excellent value, because the frame and mechanism are built for commercial use and many wear parts can be replaced. The key is buying from a seller who verifies authenticity, tests every adjustment and discloses wear honestly. Inspect the arm caps, seat foam, cylinder, casters and fabric before you buy.

What is the difference between Leap V1 and Leap V2?

Leap V2 is the later generation of the chair. It refined the adjustment controls, including the seat depth mechanism and the arms, and updated the overall design. V1 chairs can still be comfortable, but most buyers looking at pre-owned Leaps prefer V2 for its controls and adjustability.

How can I tell if a Leap is V1 or V2?

Start with the manufacturer label under the seat, which carries model and manufacturing information. Then compare the seat depth controls, the arm adjustments and any control labeling against current Steelcase product documentation. If a seller cannot show you a clear label photo, ask more questions before buying.

How do I spot a fake Steelcase Leap?

Check for an intact manufacturer label under the seat, consistent build quality, smooth and complete adjustments, and a price that makes sense for the chair's condition. Replicas and look-alike chairs circulate in the consumer market, so buy from a seller who inspects labels and construction before listing.

Does the Steelcase warranty transfer to a used Leap?

Generally, no. Manufacturer warranties are typically written for the original purchaser and do not transfer to pre-owned buyers. Check Steelcase's current warranty terms, and ask any reseller in writing what they stand behind.

What wears out on a used Leap chair?

The most common wear points are the arm caps, seat foam, gas cylinder, casters and upholstery fabric. Most of these are replaceable, so wear is not automatically a dealbreaker, but it should be reflected in the condition grade and price.
