Office Furniture Disposal, Recycling and E-Waste: A Responsible Guide

When an office closes, every chair, panel, monitor and hard drive has to go somewhere. This guide explains the disposal hierarchy, what is really recyclable, how data-bearing devices should be handled and what documentation to demand from your vendor.

11 minute read  ·  Updated September 2026

Office furniture disposal sounds simple until you face a full floor. There are workstations with steel frames and fabric panels, conference tables made of laminate over particleboard, task chairs with foam and plastic, and a pile of computers, monitors, phones and printers, some of which still hold company data. Throwing it all in a dumpster is expensive, wasteful and, for the electronics, potentially a security and compliance problem.

This guide explains how responsible disposal works for commercial furniture and office e-waste. It is written for facilities managers, IT leaders, sustainability teams and office managers who need to clear a space and document how it was done.

The disposal hierarchy

The most useful way to think about office furniture disposal is as a ladder. Each step down usually recovers less value and creates more waste. The goal is to move every item as high up the ladder as its condition allows.

  1. Resale. Furniture with market demand is sold for reuse, often after cleaning or refurbishment. Resale recovers value that can offset the cost of the whole project.
  2. Reuse and donation. Items without much resale value but still usable go to schools, nonprofits, reuse organizations or employees.
  3. Recycling. Items that cannot be reused are broken down so materials such as steel and aluminum can be recovered.
  4. Landfill. Only what has no reasonable reuse or recycling path is disposed of.

What is recyclable in office and systems furniture

Commercial furniture is made from mixed materials. Some separate and recycle easily. Others do not. Knowing the difference helps you set realistic expectations for diversion.

Usually recyclable

  • Steel. Panel frames, workstation supports, filing cabinets, lateral files, pedestals, shelving and many table bases. Steel is commonly accepted as scrap metal.
  • Aluminum. Chair bases, some trim, frames and extrusions.
  • Some plastics. Certain chair shells and components may be recyclable if they can be separated and identified, though acceptance varies by local recycler.
  • Cardboard and packaging. Generated during disassembly and moving.

Harder to recycle

  • Fabric-wrapped panels. Fabric is typically bonded to an acoustic core, which makes separation labor-intensive.
  • Laminate over particleboard. Common in worksurfaces and casegoods. Recycling options exist in some areas but are limited.
  • Upholstered seating. Foam, fabric, plastic and metal are combined, and separating them takes time.
  • Glass and mixed-material tiles. Acceptance depends on local facilities.

This is why resale matters so much for systems furniture. A matched set of workstations kept intact and reinstalled elsewhere avoids the recycling problem entirely. When stations cannot be resold, disassembling them to recover the steel still diverts a meaningful share of the material from landfill.

Office e-waste disposal

Office electronics should never go into a furniture dumpster. Many devices contain materials that do not belong in landfill, and state and local rules in many areas restrict how certain electronics are discarded. Rules vary, so confirm the requirements where your office is located.

Common office e-waste

  • Desktop computers, laptops and docking stations
  • Monitors and televisions
  • Servers, network switches, routers and firewalls
  • Desk phones and conference phones
  • Printers, copiers and scanners, some of which contain internal drives
  • External drives, USB drives, backup tapes and memory cards
  • Batteries, including UPS units
  • Cables, power strips and peripherals

How e-waste should be handled

  1. Inventory. Record devices by type, and by serial number or asset tag for anything that stores data.
  2. Coordinate with IT. Your IT team decides what is redeployed, what is returned to lessors and what is retired.
  3. Separate data-bearing devices. Keep them in a controlled area until they are sanitized or destroyed.
  4. Use a qualified recycler. Ask your vendor where electronics go and how they are processed.
  5. Collect documentation. Recycling receipts and certificates of data destruction belong in your records.
Check for leased equipment. Copiers, printers and some computers are often leased. Returning them may be required under the lease agreement, and leased copiers may contain drives with data. Confirm ownership before anything is scheduled for recycling.

Data-bearing devices and certificates of data destruction

This section gives general descriptions, not security or compliance advice. Your IT, security and legal teams should set the standard your organization needs.

What counts as data-bearing

Any device with storage can hold sensitive information: hard drives and solid-state drives in computers and servers, drives inside copiers and multifunction printers, phones, tablets, network equipment with stored configurations, and removable media.

Sanitization vs physical destruction

  • Sanitization (wiping). Software or firmware-based methods overwrite or erase data so it is not reasonably recoverable. The right method depends on the media type. Solid-state drives, for example, behave differently from traditional spinning hard drives. Verified sanitization can allow a device to be reused or resold.
  • Physical destruction. Media is shredded, crushed, drilled or otherwise destroyed so it cannot be used again. This removes the reuse option but gives a visible, final result that many organizations prefer for highly sensitive data.

NIST SP 800-88

NIST Special Publication 800-88, Guidelines for Media Sanitization, published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology, is a commonly referenced guideline for media sanitization. It describes categories of sanitization, often summarized as clear, purge and destroy, and discusses matching the method to the media type and data sensitivity. It is a guideline, not a certification. When a vendor mentions it, ask how their process aligns with it and what verification and documentation they provide.

What a certificate of data destruction should include

  • Your company name and project location
  • A list of devices or media, ideally by serial number or asset tag
  • The method used, such as sanitization or physical destruction
  • The date of sanitization or destruction
  • The name of the vendor and the responsible individual
  • A statement of chain of custody from pickup to completion

Diversion summaries for ESG reporting

A diversion summary shows where the materials from your project went. Sustainability and ESG teams often use it to report on waste and circularity efforts, and finance teams use it to explain disposition decisions.

What a useful summary includes

  • Project name, location and dates
  • Categories of items handled
  • How each category was handled: resold, donated or reused, recycled, or landfilled
  • Quantities by item count, weight or estimated weight, with the basis stated
  • E-waste handling and reference to certificates of data destruction

Keep the reporting honest

Ask how quantities are measured. Some figures come from scale tickets, while others are estimates based on item counts and typical weights. Both can be useful, but your report should say which is which. Avoid publishing diversion percentages you cannot support with records.

What to ask disposal vendors

Many vendors say they recycle. Fewer can explain exactly how. These questions separate them.

  • Will you evaluate resale value before disposal? A vendor focused only on hauling has no reason to.
  • Do you take everything or only certain items? Leftovers become your problem.
  • Where do recyclable metals go? Ask for the category of facility and receipts.
  • How do you handle e-waste? Ask which recyclers are used and how devices are tracked.
  • What happens to data-bearing devices? Ask about sanitization or destruction methods, chain of custody and certificates.
  • What is in your diversion summary? Ask for a sample.
  • Can you meet building requirements? COIs, freight elevator bookings, dock times and night or weekend work.
  • What does the price include? Disassembly, removal, cabling, debris and final cleaning should be spelled out.
Ask for the paperwork before you sign. A sample certificate of data destruction and a sample diversion summary tell you more about a vendor than a sustainability claim on a website.

Putting it into a project plan

  1. Inventory furniture and electronics, and note which are owned or leased.
  2. Tag keepers for relocation to another office.
  3. Get a walkthrough and written offer covering resale, removal and disposal.
  4. Set the e-waste standard with IT, including sanitization or destruction requirements.
  5. Choose donation recipients for selected items, if any.
  6. Schedule removal around building rules and your deadline.
  7. Collect closeout documents: certificates of data destruction and a diversion summary.

Frequently asked questions

How do I dispose of office furniture responsibly?

Follow the disposal hierarchy: resell what has value, donate or reuse what someone can use, recycle materials that have a recycling path, and send only what remains to landfill. Use one vendor who can sort items into those lanes and document the results. Ask for a diversion summary at the end of the project.

Is office furniture recyclable?

Much of it is, at least in part. Steel frames, panel frames, filing cabinets and aluminum components are commonly recyclable as scrap metal. Fabric-wrapped panels, laminate over particleboard, foam and mixed-material components are harder to recycle and may end up in landfill if they cannot be resold or reused.

How do I dispose of office electronics and e-waste?

Use an e-waste recycler or a vendor that works with one, rather than a general junk hauler. Many electronics contain materials that should not go to landfill, and some state and local rules restrict how they are discarded. Devices that store data should be sanitized or destroyed with documentation before or during recycling.

What is a certificate of data destruction?

A certificate of data destruction is a document from a vendor stating that data on specific devices or media was sanitized or physically destroyed. A useful certificate identifies the devices, often by serial number or asset tag, states the method used, gives the date and names the party responsible. Keep it with your IT asset disposition records.

What is NIST SP 800-88?

NIST Special Publication 800-88, Guidelines for Media Sanitization, is a widely referenced U.S. government guideline describing methods for sanitizing storage media, such as clearing, purging and destroying. Many organizations and vendors use it as a reference point for data sanitization practices. It is a guideline, not a certification, so ask vendors how their process aligns with it and what documentation they provide.

Is wiping a hard drive enough, or should it be destroyed?

It depends on the media type, the sensitivity of the data and your organization's policies. Properly performed and verified sanitization can allow some devices to be reused or resold, while physical destruction makes the media unusable. Your IT or security team should set the standard, and your vendor should document which method was applied to each device.

What is a diversion summary?

A diversion summary is a closeout report showing how the items from your project were handled, such as resold, donated, recycled or sent to landfill. It is often used for internal sustainability or ESG reporting. Ask your vendor what the summary includes and how quantities are measured or estimated before the project starts.
