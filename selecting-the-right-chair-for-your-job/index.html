Selecting the Right Chair for Your Job

The right chair depends on how you work, not just how it looks. Match the job to the chair type, check the fit, then pick a condition grade that fits your budget.

6 minute read

Most people choose an office chair the way they choose a couch: by sitting in it for ten seconds and deciding it feels fine. But a chair that feels fine for ten seconds can be miserable by hour six. The better approach is to start with the job. A claims processor, a receptionist, a design engineer and a manager who lives in meetings all need something different. This guide shows you how to match the work to the chair, what to check for fit, and how pre-owned condition grades fit into the decision.

Ergonomic task chairs: where to spend

If a job keeps someone seated most of the day, the task chair is the most important piece of furniture they touch. This is where pre-owned designer seating shines. Many well-known models from Herman Miller, Steelcase, Haworth and Humanscale were engineered for years of commercial use, so a pre-owned chair can still offer the full adjustment range at a lower price than new.

Common model families to know

  • Herman Miller Aeron and Mirra are mesh chairs known for breathability and posture support. Aeron comes in sizes A, B and C, so check sizing along with condition.
  • Steelcase Leap and Gesture are upholstered chairs with highly adjustable backs and arms, popular for long computer days.
  • Haworth Zody is known for its adjustable lumbar and pelvic support.
  • Humanscale Freedom uses a self-adjusting recline that suits people who prefer fewer knobs.

The five-minute fit check

A chair only works if it fits the person sitting in it. Run through this before you buy one chair or a hundred.

  1. Seat height. Feet flat on the floor, thighs roughly level, knees near 90 degrees.
  2. Seat depth. Two to three fingers of space between the seat edge and the back of your knees.
  3. Lumbar support. The curve should meet the small of your back, not your ribs or hips.
  4. Armrests. Shoulders relaxed, elbows near 90 degrees, arms low enough to slide under the desk.
  5. Recline. You should be able to lean back without being pushed forward or falling back too easily.
  6. Desk height. Check the chair with the actual work surface. A great chair at the wrong desk still causes strain. Adjustable keyboard trays or sit-stand desks can close the gap.
Size is not condition. A chair listed as "size B" and a chair listed as "Grade B" are two different things. Size describes the frame dimensions of certain models. Condition grade describes wear. Confirm both on every order so a large team does not end up in chairs that do not fit.

Choosing a condition grade

Office Station grades pre-owned furniture as A like new, B very good and C good. For seating, grade mostly reflects how the chair looks rather than whether it works. Use it to spend where people notice.

  • Grade A like new for executive offices, reception and client-facing conference rooms.
  • Grade B very good for most task seating in open plans and private offices. It is often the best balance of price and appearance.
  • Grade C good for training rooms, overflow seating, warehouses and call centers where durability matters more than a perfect finish.

Whatever the grade, test the controls: height should hold without sinking, tilt should lock, arms should adjust smoothly and casters should roll freely. Rolling out chairs to a whole team

Buying for a department is different from buying for yourself. A few practical rules help:

  • Standardize on one or two task chair models so controls are familiar and spare parts are easier.
  • Offer an alternate size or a model with a wider seat for people the standard chair does not fit.
  • Match chair arms to desk and workstation heights before ordering in volume.
  • Plan chairs alongside workstations. If you are also pricing used cubicles, chairs should be part of the same layout conversation.

Space planning is free, and it is a good moment to confirm clearances so chairs can roll back without hitting panels or file cabinets.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose an office chair for long workdays?

Look for adjustable seat height, seat depth, lumbar support, armrests and a recline tension you can set. Sit in the chair for several minutes with your feet flat and thighs level. If you spend six or more hours seated, full adjustability is worth prioritizing over style.

Are pre-owned ergonomic chairs a good value?

They can be. Designer task chairs from brands like Herman Miller, Steelcase, Haworth and Humanscale are built for years of commercial use, and pre-owned pricing is well below new. Check the condition grade and test every adjustment, and keep in mind manufacturer warranties generally do not transfer to pre-owned chairs.

What do condition grades A, B and C mean for chairs?

Grade A is like new, Grade B is very good and Grade C is good. For chairs, the grade mostly reflects visible wear on upholstery, mesh, arms and the base. Every grade should still adjust and function properly, so test the mechanisms either way.

Is the size on a chair the same as its condition grade?

No. Some chairs, including the Herman Miller Aeron, come in sizes labeled A, B and C, where B is the medium size. That is separate from a condition grade of A, B or C, so confirm both when you order.

Do conference rooms need ergonomic chairs?

Not always. Short meetings work fine with simpler conference chairs that swivel and roll. If the room hosts long working sessions, trainings or all-day reviews, choose chairs with a reclining back and adjustable height.
