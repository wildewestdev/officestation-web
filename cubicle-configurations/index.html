Cubicle Configurations: Layouts, Tradeoffs and Real Pricing

The same 6x6 workstation can sit in a row, a pod of four or a long spine, and each choice changes privacy, headcount and cost. Compare the common cubicle configurations and price them with real numbers.

6 minute read

Buyers often start a cubicle project by asking about size. The better first question is configuration. How workstations connect to each other decides how many people fit, how private each seat feels, where power runs and how easily you can rearrange later. This guide walks through the cubicle configurations you will see on almost any floor plan, then prices them using real 6x6 numbers.

The building blocks

Every configuration is assembled from the same parts: panels or frames, work surfaces, storage, and power and data. Two choices within each station shape everything else.

Work surface shape

  • Straight. One rectangular surface. Compact and cheap, but little room to spread out.
  • L-shape. A corner surface that fits a monitor setup on one leg and paperwork on the other. The most common shape in a 6x6.
  • U-shape. Three surfaces. Useful for roles that juggle equipment and documents, but it usually needs a larger footprint than 6x6.

Panel height

  • Low. Open sightlines and more daylight across the floor.
  • Mid. Seated privacy while standing people can still see across.
  • High. The most visual and acoustic separation, best for call-heavy work.

Match the configuration to the column grid. Pods of four usually fit neatly between building columns, while long back-to-back rows work best on open, column-free floors. Measure your column spacing before choosing, and let free space planning test both options.

How to choose a configuration

  1. Group by team. People who collaborate daily belong in the same pod or row segment.
  2. Sort by noise. Put phone-heavy roles in higher-panel stations away from quiet focus work.
  3. Find your power. Existing floor boxes, power poles or wall outlets often decide where rows and spines can go. Electrical changes are billed separately from furniture.
  4. Keep circulation clear. Plan main aisles first, then fill the space around them, and confirm code and accessibility clearances.
  5. Leave room to grow. Rows and spines are easy to extend. Pods add capacity in blocks.

Which tier fits which configuration

Liquidation-grade stations can be in stock in as little as 72 hours. They suit fast openings, overflow areas and temporary project teams. The catch is availability: a large pod layout needs enough matching components from one product line, so confirm quantities early.

Remanufactured stations usually take 7 to 14 days. They are the middle ground for departments that want a consistent look without new pricing, and they make larger matched configurations easier to fulfill.

New stations take 4 to 6 weeks and offer the widest choice of finishes and panel options. They make sense when the configuration must match a specific design standard.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most common cubicle configurations?

The most common are single rows against a wall, back-to-back rows, pods or clusters of two to eight stations, spine layouts that run stations off a central power wall, and individual L-shape or U-shape stations. Most offices combine two or three of these to fit the floor plan.

How much does a 6x6 cubicle cost?

At Office Station, a 6'x6' workstation, furniture only, is $615 liquidation-grade, $1,245 remanufactured and $1,920 new. Prices exclude electrical, delivery and professional installation. Use the quote builder at /used-cubicles/#quote for your configuration.

Are pods cheaper than individual cubicles?

Pods share panels between neighbors, which can reduce the total panel count compared with freestanding stations. The actual savings depend on the product line and configuration, so compare quotes for the specific layout rather than assuming a percentage.

Is there a discount for larger cubicle orders?

Yes. Volume pricing is 5% off at 20 or more stations, 8% at 50 or more, and 12% at 100 or more stations.

What panel height should I choose?

Lower panels, roughly desk height to seated eye level, keep sightlines open and make a floor feel brighter. Taller panels give more visual and acoustic privacy for phone-heavy roles. Many offices use low panels on the aisle side and taller panels between neighbors.
