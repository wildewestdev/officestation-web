How to Create Collaboration Areas in an Office

Good collaboration areas get used every day. Poor ones become storage. Here is how to choose, size and place collaboration zones using benching, tables and a real floor plan.

7 minute read

Almost every office wants more collaboration space. The trouble is that many collaboration areas are designed around a photo instead of a behavior. A lounge corner nobody uses, a giant table in a room that only ever hosts two people, a benching row placed right next to the finance team that needs quiet. This guide helps you build zones people actually use.

Start with how your team works

Before choosing furniture, spend a week or two noting how people collaborate. Look for patterns in three areas:

  • Group size. Are most conversations between two or three people, or do full teams of eight gather often?
  • Duration. Quick five-minute check-ins need different furniture than half-day working sessions.
  • Tools. Do people share a screen, sketch on a whiteboard, or just talk?

A common finding is that most meetings are small and short, while most conference rooms are built for large groups. That mismatch is your opportunity.

Place zones to protect focus work

Placement is where collaboration areas succeed or fail. Noise travels, and a busy zone in the wrong spot will frustrate everyone nearby.

  1. Put active zones near movement. Entries, kitchens, printers and main aisles already carry noise and foot traffic.
  2. Keep quiet work at the edges. Rows of focus workstations do better away from circulation, often along windows or in corners.
  3. Build buffers. Storage cabinets, higher-panel workstations or a row of lockers between loud and quiet areas softens sound and signals the change in use.
  4. Keep zones close to the teams that use them. A great project table on the other side of the floor will go unused.
  5. Plan power and data early. Collaboration without a place to plug in a laptop does not last. Electrical work is a separate cost from furniture, so budget for it.
Convert, do not just add. You rarely need more square footage for collaboration. Swapping a few oversized workstations for benching, or turning an underused private office into a huddle room, often creates the space you need without a move.

Size tables and benching correctly

Undersized tables crowd people, and oversized ones waste floor area. Use these practical rules as a starting point, then confirm them on your floor plan.

  • Allow roughly 24 to 30 inches of table edge per person seated at a table.
  • Leave enough room behind chairs for people to pull out and pass behind without squeezing, and more where the space behind is also an aisle.
  • Standing-height tables need less chair clearance but more open floor around them.
  • For benching, check that each person has enough surface for their monitor and work, and that the shared spine can carry power.
  • Round tables encourage equal conversation. Rectangular tables suit screen sharing at one end.

Accessibility and fire code clearances apply to all of these, so check them against your building's requirements.

Collaboration area checklist

  • Observed group sizes, meeting length and tools used
  • Two or three zone types matched to those patterns
  • Active zones near circulation, quiet work at the edges
  • Buffers between loud and quiet areas
  • Power and data planned and budgeted separately
  • Tables and benching sized per person with clearances confirmed
  • Layout tested on your floor plan through free space planning

Frequently asked questions

How much of an office should be collaboration space?

There is no single right ratio. It depends on how your team works. Start by tracking how often people meet and in what group sizes, then build zones for those patterns. Many offices add collaboration space by converting underused private offices or oversized workstation rows.

Where should collaboration areas go?

Place active collaboration zones near circulation paths, kitchens or entries and away from rows of heads-down workstations. Use storage, a change in flooring or a buffer zone to separate noisy areas from quiet ones.

What is benching and does it work for collaboration?

Benching is a shared, open desk system where several people sit along one long work surface. It works well for project teams that talk throughout the day. It is less suited to people who need privacy or long periods of quiet focus.

Can I build collaboration areas with pre-owned furniture?
