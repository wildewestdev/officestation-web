Open Office Layout: How to Plan Benching and Cubicles by Budget Tier

A good open office layout starts with headcount, aisles and how people actually work. Then you pick the furniture tier that fits your budget and your move-in date.

6 minute read

An open office layout is not just desks in a big room. It is a set of decisions about density, circulation, noise, power and budget that all affect each other. Get the order right and the furniture choice becomes easy. Get it wrong and you end up with stations that block aisles or panels that do not reach the floor boxes.

This guide walks through the planning sequence, compares benching and cubicles, and shows what the same layout costs at liquidation-grade, remanufactured and new pricing.

Start with the floor, not the furniture

Before you pick a station style, gather four things:

  1. A dimensioned floor plan. Include columns, windows, doors, restrooms and the core. Columns are the most common reason a row of stations comes up one seat short.
  2. Power and data locations. Note floor boxes, wall outlets and any ceiling feeds. Panel systems and benching both need a path for power, and moving a floor box is expensive.
  3. Headcount today and in 18 months. Plan for growth now. Adding a station to the end of a run later is simple. Reworking a whole row is not.
  4. How each team works. Count who is on the phone all day, who needs file storage, who is hybrid and who collaborates at the desk.

Plan circulation before seats

Aisles are what make an open plan feel open. Lay them out first, then fill the remaining zones with stations.

  • Primary aisles connect entries, exits, the kitchen and restrooms. Plan these at roughly 5 to 6 feet so two people can pass comfortably.
  • Secondary aisles run between rows of stations. Plan at least 42 to 48 inches so a seated person can push back while someone walks behind them.
  • Accessible routes must meet current ADA and local code requirements. Check with your building and your permit reviewer, since egress rules can override a layout you like.
Measure chair clearance. The most common open-plan mistake is ignoring the space behind the chair. Budget about 3 feet from the work surface edge before any aisle begins, or people will bump chairs all day.

Benching vs cubicles

Most open plans use one of these two families, or a mix of both. Here is how they compare.

FactorBenchingCubicles (6'x6' panel stations)
DensityHighest. Seats share a spine and sit face to face.Lower. Each station has its own footprint.
Privacy and acousticsMinimal. Low screens at best.Better. Panel height controls sightlines and some sound.
StorageUsually a mobile pedestal or shared lockers.Room for pedestals, lateral files and overheads.
PowerRuns through a shared beam or spine.Runs through panel raceways.
ReconfigurationFast. Add or remove seats along the run.Moderate. Panels and connectors must match.
Best forProject teams, hybrid seating, collaboration.Call-heavy roles, paperwork, focused work.

A practical pattern: put benching near the windows and collaboration zones where people move around, and put panel stations deeper in the floor where focus work happens. Taller panels near the core also help block noise from kitchens and copy areas.

Panel height matters more than you think

Low panels around 47 inches keep sightlines open and let daylight travel. Mid-height panels around 53 inches give seated privacy while standing people can still see across the room. Tall panels feel closed and cut daylight, so save them for spots that truly need them. If you are mixing pre-owned stations, confirm panel heights and connector types match before you plan a shared run.

Mixing tiers on one floor

You do not have to pick one tier for everyone. A common approach is liquidation-grade stations for a fast-growing team that needs seats this week, remanufactured stations for the main floor, and new pieces only where a specific finish or configuration is required. Keep finishes and panel heights consistent within a single run so the space still looks planned.

Open office layout checklist

  • Dimensioned floor plan with columns and core marked
  • Power and data locations confirmed with the building
  • Primary and secondary aisle widths drawn before seats
  • About 3 feet of chair clearance behind every work surface
  • Teams zoned by work style: collaboration, calls, focus
  • Panel heights and connector types matched within each run
  • Storage counted per person, not guessed
  • Tier chosen per zone based on budget and move-in date
  • Electrical, delivery and installation budgeted separately
  • Growth seats planned at the ends of runs

Frequently asked questions

How much space does an open office layout need per person?

Most open plans land somewhere between 100 and 150 usable square feet per person once you count aisles, collaboration areas and storage. Benching sits at the dense end of that range and 6'x6' cubicles at the roomier end. Your final number depends on how many meeting rooms and shared zones you carve out of the same floor.

Should I choose benching or cubicles?

Benching suits teams that collaborate constantly, move often and do not need much personal storage. Cubicles with panels suit roles that take calls, handle paperwork or need focus. Many offices mix both, with benching for project teams and panel stations for heads-down work.
